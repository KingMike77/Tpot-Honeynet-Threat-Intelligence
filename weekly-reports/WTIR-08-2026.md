## Executive Summary

> **At a Glance**
> - **Total events:** 702,000, down about 20% week over week, the largest week over week decline recorded in the series
> - **Top actor:** 193.46.255.112 (UNMANAGED LTD, AS47890) with 165,061 hits, or 23.5% of all traffic
> - **Recurring actor:** Alsycon B.V./HostUS Solutions (AS49870) ran two IP clusters simultaneously this week, 45.95.147.229 tagged Netherlands and a 160.119.76.0/24 cluster tagged Seychelles, together accounting for at least 23,587 hits and reproducing the multi country attribution mismatch first documented in WTIR-05
> - **Flagship finding:** two Ciscoasa scanning clusters, AS211486 tagged Georgia and AS205997 tagged Kazakhstan, share a domain identifier and an identical AbuseIPDB first report date, indicating shared infrastructure and very likely a shared operator running both
> - **Ciscoasa spike:** verified clean at 109,000 hits, 15.5% share, using the spike check method established for ConPot in WTIR-06 and WTIR-07

The honeynet logged 702,000 events this week, about 20% fewer than last week. The interesting part was not the decline. It was the Ciscoasa spike hiding inside it.

After catching two separate ConPot logging bugs in the previous two reports, I have made it standard practice not to trust a sudden spike until I verify it myself. I broke Ciscoasa's traffic down by source IP and checked whether one attacker was responsible for a disproportionate share of the records. It held up. There were 243 distinct source IPs involved, no single source was inflating the count, and the volume was genuine attacker traffic.

What started as a routine data quality check turned into this report's central investigation. Two Ciscoasa clusters, tagged to countries I had never seen in my top 10 before, turned out to share multiple infrastructure indicators, an unusual and telling connection to chase down.

At the same time, the series' two established recurring actors both had significant weeks. UNMANAGED LTD, AS47890, based in Romania and first profiled in WTIR-07, returned and posted the largest single actor volume this series has recorded. Alsycon B.V./HostUS Solutions, AS49870, first seen in WTIR-02, did something more interesting than simply returning. Its original IP came back tagged Netherlands, while its familiar WTIR-05/07 sibling IP was active in parallel tagged Seychelles, the same multi country fracturing this operator has shown before.

## What I Changed This Week

A few habits changed this week, and each one grew directly out of a lesson from the last two reports.

**Spike checking stopped being a ConPot only step.** The two logging bugs I found there, one a per varbind fan out issue in WTIR-06 and one a duplicate write bug in WTIR-07, taught me that any sudden volume jump deserves verification before it is treated as real. When Ciscoasa spiked, I ran the same unique source IP breakdown I use on ConPot. I filtered the honeypot's traffic, grouped it by source IP, and checked whether one attacker was responsible for a disproportionate share of records. It came back clean, which gave me the confidence to actually investigate the spike instead of flagging it as a possible artifact.

**I started cross referencing infrastructure identifiers instead of trusting GeoIP alone.** Two attackers can look completely unrelated on a map and still turn out to be the same operator. This week I checked whether two similarly shaped actors shared a domain, an abuse contact, or a reporting timeline, rather than assuming that different country tags meant different people. That check is exactly what surfaced the Georgia and Kazakhstan link, and it is now a standard step any time two actors' behavior looks suspiciously alike.

**RIPE Atlas became my tiebreaker for geolocation disputes.** When GeoIP, WHOIS, and reputation lookups disagree on where an actor actually sits, I now pull physical RIPE Atlas probe locations registered under the same ASN, since those are real network vantage points rather than database entries. I used this twice this week. It settled one actor's location definitively in Romania and reinforced another's in China, even while a third actor's location stayed genuinely unresolved.

**I started categorizing actors by infrastructure type rather than by volume alone.** A rented, professionally run scanning operation and a single compromised residential device can generate similar hit counts while representing very different problems. This week made that distinction concrete, since four of the five profiled actors are dedicated rented infrastructure with multi year abuse histories, while one looks like a hijacked consumer device.

## Methodology

This report covers telemetry collected by a public facing T-Pot honeynet over a seven day period from August 1 through August 8, 2026. Sources include Elastic and Kibana dashboards and T-Pot honeypot logs, cross referenced against Hurricane Electric's BGP Toolkit, including RIPE RDAP and WHOIS lookups, along with RIPE Atlas, Shodan, VirusTotal, AbuseIPDB, GreyNoise, Cisco Talos, and OTX AlienVault.

**Spike validation.** Following the ConPot logging inflation defects found in WTIR-06 and WTIR-07, any honeypot showing a sudden week over week volume increase is now checked for single source fan out, meaning one IP responsible for a disproportionate share of records, before it is treated as genuine rather than a logging artifact.

**Recurrence checks.** Every candidate IP and its ASN are checked against prior WTIR reports for overlap before a profile is finalized.

**Confidence ratings.** Every finding in this report receives a confidence tier, because some conclusions are much better supported than others.

| Tier | Criteria | Example basis |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Directly observed in this honeynet's own telemetry, and corroborated by 2 or more independent external sources | GreyNoise classification, AbuseIPDB report history, and RIPE registration data all agreeing |
| **Medium** | Directly observed in this honeynet's telemetry, but corroborated by only one external source, or the sources disagree on a minor detail | A GreyNoise tag confirms behavior, but AbuseIPDB has too few reports to independently verify scale |
| **Low** | A plausible explanation consistent with the available data, but resting on inference rather than direct confirmation | A hostname pattern suggests a link between two actors that has not been independently verified |

A rating is assigned per finding rather than per IP. The same actor can carry a High confidence rating for the claim that it is a scanner, while a related claim, such as attribution to a specific operator, might only warrant Medium or Low.

## Investigation 1: The Toolkit for Hire, Round Two (Romania)

**193.46.255.112 (UNMANAGED LTD, AS47890), a recurring actor now in its second consecutive week.**

This IP was profiled in WTIR-07 as a broad spectrum opportunistic scanner. It came back this week and came back loud. It logged 165,061 hits, which is 23.5% of all traffic and the largest single actor volume this series has recorded to date. The overwhelming majority of that traffic, roughly 162,000 hits, landed against the Honeytrap honeypot alone, with the remaining few hundred hits scattered thinly across Cowrie, Dionaea, Mailoney, ConPot, and several others.

![WTIR-08 Romania Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Romania%20Dashboard.png)

The port list matches the broad spectrum label it earned last week, even though almost all of it funneled through one honeypot. It includes port 25 for SMTP, port 6379 for Redis, port 443 for HTTPS, port 50100, and port 2404, which is used by IEC 60870-5-104, an industrial SCADA protocol. That last one is worth pausing on. This actor is not specializing in one target class. It is probing whatever is listening, including at least one industrial control probe alongside the usual mail and cache ports, and Honeytrap, a generic low interaction honeypot that catches connection attempts across a wide range of TCP ports, is what ends up capturing most of that spread in a single bucket.

This actor's own Suricata telemetry, not just outside reporting, confirms it is exploiting CVE-2018-13379, the FortiOS SSL VPN path traversal vulnerability that allows an unauthenticated attacker to read session files off a vulnerable device and potentially harvest credentials for further access. The Suricata CVE panel logged 3,655 hits against this IP tied directly to that CVE, and two exploit classified signatures fired specifically on it, ET EXPLOIT FortiOS SSL VPN Information Disclosure (CVE-2018-13379) and ET EXPLOIT Fortinet FortiOS/FortiProxy SSL VPN Web Portal Path Traversal.

![WTIR-08 Romania Suricata CVE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Romania%20Suricata%20CVE.png)

The Suricata Alert Category Histogram for this IP shows Attempted Administrator Privilege Gain and Attempted Denial of Service as active alert categories throughout the week, with a sharp spike to roughly 25,000 alerts on August 4th. This is not a scanner limited to reconnaissance. The honeynet's own IDS caught it attempting to escalate access and disrupt availability on whatever it could reach, not just fingerprint it. Raw request logs from this IP also show it probing for exposed Asterisk PBX configuration files and generic PHP version disclosure endpoints, `/admin/config.php`, `/_asterisk/phpversions.php`, `/graph.php`, further evidence of the same opportunistic, whatever is listening approach documented above.

On paper, the registrant is UNMANAGED LTD, a UK registered company with registration number 12461131 and a registered address in Rushden, England. In practice, the infrastructure runs through a Romanian reseller. RIPE's maintainer field, `ro-btel2-1-mnt`, points to Bunea Telecom SRL, and the netblock itself has been active since October 2020. AbuseIPDB shows 319 reports from 102 distinct sources dating back to April 30, 2021, which is over five years of documented port scanning, SSH and Telnet brute forcing, SQL injection, and FTP abuse, reported by sensors across Germany, France, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia. VirusTotal has six vendors flagging it malicious, including Certego, CRDF, Forcepoint ThreatSeeker, Fortinet, GreyNoise, and SOCRadar.

I wanted another independent signal for the location question, so I checked RIPE Atlas. Every probe registered under this actor's ASN, including probes 1014087, 1014089, and 1014090, plus a Globalping vantage point, sits in Timișoara, Timiș County, Romania. Every other source agrees. This provided an independent network-level signal that supported the Romanian attribution, and it is a useful contrast to the mess that shows up in Investigation 3.

One more detail is worth noting. The wider /24 block also functions as a bulk-mail sending range with poor sender reputation across most of its IPs. That dual use pattern, in which scanning infrastructure and mail infrastructure sit on the same block, is consistent with commodity hosting that can be rented for multiple purposes, including scanning and spam activity.

**Assessment. Confidence: High.** This is a long running, five-plus-year, broad-spectrum actor, confirmed via this honeynet's own Suricata telemetry to be attempting actual exploitation of CVE-2018-13379, not just scanning for it, and its Romanian location is independently supported by RIPE Atlas network measurement data.

## Investigation 2: Same Actor, Two Costumes at Once (Netherlands and Seychelles)

**45.95.147.229 and 160.119.76.106, both Alsycon B.V./HostUS Solutions, AS49870. This operator is not just recurring, it is running two separate IP clusters at the same time this week.**

This IP started the series' longest-running infrastructure story. 45.95.147.229 first appeared in WTIR-02, then went quiet while a sibling IP, 160.119.76.106, carried the campaign through WTIR-05 and WTIR-07. When I first drafted this section, I read this week's data as the original IP simply returning after a quiet stretch. That was not the full picture.

![WTIR-08 Netherlands Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Netherlands%20Dashboard.png)

I did not specifically check on Seychelles traffic at first. It only came up because Seychelles appeared in the top 10 attacking countries again this week, and given its history in this series, that was enough to make me check it out specifically to see whether the same threat actor from before had returned. Back in WTIR-05, Seychelles was one of three country tags, alongside the Netherlands and South Africa, that this same operator's infrastructure showed up under simultaneously. That was the first time I learned that a single ASN's traffic can fracture across multiple GeoIP country labels depending on which sub-range gets hit, rather than one IP always mapping cleanly to one country. So when Seychelles showed up in the top 10 again this week, I wanted to know if it was the same pattern repeating. It was, and with the same operator behind it. The top attacker turned out to be Alsycon B.V. again. The Attacker AS/N panel for the Seychelles filtered dashboard shows AS49870 at 9,956 hits, and the top source IP is 160.119.76.106 at 7,650 hits, the exact sibling IP from WTIR-05 and WTIR-07. Two more IPs in the same /24 show up right behind it, 160.119.76.18 at 2,054 hits and 160.119.76.100 at 200 hits.

![WTIR-08 Seychelles Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Seychelles%20Dashboard.png)

That changes the recurrence table. This operator ran two separate IP clusters simultaneously this week under two different country tags, not one IP quietly returning after an absence.

| Report | Period | IP | Country Tag | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WTIR-02 | approximately June 2026 | 45.95.147.229 | Netherlands | Original appearance |
| WTIR-05 | Jul 11 to 18 | 160.119.76.106 | multi country, mismatch documented | Enterprise VPN and firewall toolkit targeting FortiOS, Palo Alto, Ivanti, Pulse Secure, F5, Sophos XG, and ConnectWise |
| WTIR-07 | Jul 25 to Aug 1 | 160.119.76.106 | Seychelles, Netherlands, and United States simultaneously | Identical toolkit, confirmed live via GreyNoise and rDNS |
| WTIR-08 | Aug 1 to 8 | 45.95.147.229 | Netherlands | Original IP returns with 13,631 hits |
| WTIR-08 | Aug 1 to 8 | 160.119.76.106 plus 160.119.76.18 and 160.119.76.100 | Seychelles | Sibling IP cluster active in parallel, 9,956 hits combined per the AS49870 total |

Combined across both clusters, this infrastructure accounts for at least 23,587 hits this week, showing why treating the two country tagged clusters as unrelated actors would understate their activity. This is the same multi country attribution fracturing documented in WTIR-05 and WTIR-07, reproducing again in this exact reporting period rather than as a one time quirk.

Port 9100, which is JetDirect and is used by network printers, shows up in the Netherlands tagged IP's targeting this week, and it matches hits logged directly against the Miniprint honeypot, alongside the usual mail, Redis, and generic scanning ports.

This IP's own Suricata telemetry also ties it to CVE-2002-0013, a long standing SNMP request handling vulnerability that lets a remote attacker cause a denial of service or gain elevated access through malformed SNMPv1 trap handling, which fits the SNMP hits logged against the ConPot honeypot from this same IP. Most of the Suricata alerts tied to this actor fall under the generic Misc Activity category, but a meaningful share are more specific, Attempted Information Leak, Bad Traffic, and Generic Protocol Command Decode. That mix reads as an actor whose traffic is mostly low signal background noise with a real exploitation attempt against an old, well documented SNMP flaw mixed in, rather than either a pure scanner or a fully targeted intrusion attempt.

Two findings this week move the HostUS relationship from inferred to confirmed. First, RIPE's organization record for Alsycon B.V. lists `MNT-HOSTUS` as an official maintainer reference. I had inferred that leasing relationship back in WTIR-05 from IPinfo enrichment data, and it is now documented in the registration itself. Second, this is the most reported actor in the entire series. AbuseIPDB shows 7,571 reports from 330 distinct sources dating to September 28, 2022, and Cisco Talos has an active malicious blocklist entry first flagged on May 25, 2023, currently set to expire on August 15, 2026, which is worth rechecking next report. VirusTotal shows 12 of 91 vendors flagging malicious, and several of them tag it specifically as Phishing, including BitDefender, G Data, SOCRadar, and VIPRE.

That last detail led somewhere unexpected. Looking at the neighboring IPs in the same /24 as 45.95.147.229, a large number carry mail sending hostnames that follow Portuguese and Mexican Spanish phishing brand naming conventions. Examples include `contaprotegida.co`, which translates to protected account, `contavalidaseg.com` and `contavalidaseg.net`, which translate to valid secure account, and `facturapagobmmx.top` and `gerandofacturapagomx.top`, which translate to invoice payment. This reads like Latin America targeted phishing mail infrastructure sharing this operator's netblock, running as a separate business in parallel to the VPN scanning this actor is known for. I have not verified this beyond the hostname pattern, so it is a lead for next report rather than a confirmed finding.

**Assessment. Confidence: High** for the recurring campaign, the confirmed HostUS relationship, and the fact that this operator ran multiple IPs under different country tags simultaneously this week. **Confidence: Medium** on the CVE-2002-0013 targeting, since the Suricata alert mix behind it is mostly generic and low signal categories rather than the named exploit signatures that backed the Romania actor's CVE confirmation. **Confidence: Low** for the LATAM phishing lead, since it is plausible but unverified beyond naming conventions.

## Investigation 3: Two Passports, One Infrastructure Cluster (Georgia and Kazakhstan)

**77.91.71.0/24 (AS211486) and 185.136.15.0/24 (AS205997), a coordinated Ciscoasa cluster.**

This is the investigation that got me curious for the same reason Seychelles did back in WTIR-05. If I am being honest, Georgia and Kazakhstan were not places I knew much about before this week, the same way I had never really thought about Seychelles until it showed up in my dashboard a few reports ago. I had never seen either country show up as a top attacking country before, and the first time an unfamiliar country lands in my top 10, I want to know why. That is the same instinct that eventually uncovered the Seychelles, Netherlands, and South Africa attribution mismatch a few reports ago, and it is what kept me pulling on this thread instead of writing it off as noise.

To put the two country tags in geographic context, here is where each country sits.

![WTIR-08 Georgia and Kazakhstan Location Map](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Georgia%20and%20Kazakhstan%20Location%20Map.png)

Georgia sits at the intersection of Eastern Europe and Western Asia, on the eastern coast of the Black Sea, bordered by Russia, Turkey, Armenia, and Azerbaijan. Kazakhstan lies much farther east in Central Asia, bordered by Russia to the north, China to the east, and the Caspian Sea to the west. Neither country has come up in this series before, which is exactly what made the traffic from both worth a second look.

One more country tag is worth flagging while I am on the subject of unfamiliar names showing up on the map. Seychelles appears in this week's Attacks by Country list again, the same country that first caught my attention back in WTIR-05 through its link to the Alsycon/HostUS attribution mismatch. Seychelles was not in the top five countries by volume this week, so it did not get an OSINT lookup during the first pass through this report, but checking it directly resolved the thread quickly, and not as a coincidence. The top ASN behind this week's Seychelles traffic is Alsycon B.V. again, running the same sibling IP, 160.119.76.106, that carried this campaign through WTIR-05 and WTIR-07. Full detail is in Investigation 2.

Before getting into the numbers, it is worth explaining what Ciscoasa actually is. It is a honeypot that emulates a Cisco ASA device, the login portal for Cisco's Adaptive Security Appliance firewalls and SSL VPN concentrators. Real Cisco ASA devices sit on the edge of enterprise networks handling remote access VPN logins, so the traffic this honeypot typically attracts is scanning for exposed ASA management interfaces and brute force attempts against the VPN login page itself, the same kind of traffic that has made real ASA vulnerabilities a popular target industry wide. In this series, Ciscoasa has never ranked in the top five honeypots by volume before. It has always been a minor, background presence next to Honeytrap, Cowrie, and Sentrypeer, which is exactly what made this week's jump into third place worth stopping on.

It started with the spike check described earlier in this report. Ciscoasa had jumped to 109,000 hits, or a 15.5% share, and I broke it down by source IP before trusting the number. There were 243 distinct IPs contributing, the top five landed within a tight band of 8,867, 8,755, 8,680, 8,679, and 8,670 hits, and there was no timestamp fan out pattern like the one that exposed the ConPot bug. The spike was genuine attacker volume. That verification is what turned this into an actual investigation once I pivoted the top contributing IPs against country data.

![WTIR-08 Ciscoasa Verification](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Ciscoasa%20Verification.png)

**The Georgia cluster.** Six IPs in a single /24 block, 77.91.71.0/24 under AS211486, were hitting Ciscoasa in near lockstep, with no single IP dominating and each landing within a few hundred hits of the others, targeting non standard high numbered ports including 8442 through 8444 and 4432 and 4433 instead of the usual 443 and 8443. That port choice suggests either evasion of default port blocklists or a deliberate sweep for ASA management interfaces exposed on non default ports, which is a real misconfiguration pattern seen in enterprise environments.

![WTIR-08 Georgia Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Georgia%20Dashboard.png)

Pinning down where this cluster actually operates from turned into a genuine mess. Kibana's GeoIP tags it Georgia. RIPE RDAP and a Talos style reputation lookup both say Israel. OTX AlienVault says Russia. AbuseIPDB and GreyNoise both say the Netherlands, specifically Amsterdam. VirusTotal says Georgia again. That is five sources with no clean consensus. Two sibling IPs in the block also carry Russian language reverse hostnames. Even the registrant name is disputed. RIPE, GreyNoise, and VirusTotal attribute the ASN to an individual named Alferov Aleksey Aleksandrovich, while a separate reputation lookup names a completely different person, sergey aleksandrovich miroshkin, as network owner on the same IP. The netblock itself was only registered on April 29, 2026, and RIPE's netname field literally reads NEW, with an abuse contact routed through `zerolimit-servers.cool` and an abuse report email of `ban-me-please@zerolimit-servers.cool`. That naming convention is a strong signal of bulletproof or no compliance hosting rather than a legitimate ISP.

**The Kazakhstan cluster.** A structurally identical pattern showed up on a separate netblock, 185.136.15.0/24 under AS205997 with the netname Vlad Cojuhari. Two heavy IPs carried most of the volume, at 8,680 and 8,650 hits, with a long tail behind them, and the cluster targeted a different but similarly non standard port range of 2443, 3443, and 34443.

![WTIR-08 Kazakhstan Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Kazakhstan%20Dashboard.png)

Here is where the two threads converged. Pulling GreyNoise data on the Kazakhstan IP, one field jumped out immediately. The domain listed was `zerolimit-servers.cool`, which is the identical domain from the Georgia netblock's RIPE abuse contact. That is not a toolkit coincidence. It is a shared infrastructure identifier.

![WTIR-08 Kazakhstan GreyNoise Domain Field](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20Kazakhstan%20GreyNoise%20Domain%20Field.png)

More corroboration followed. Both IPs were first reported on AbuseIPDB on the same date, June 3, 2026. Both carry the identical reporter count of 39 distinct sources, including the same named organizations, CELOS-SOC, NxtGenIT, EnthecSolutions, and GOLINE SA. Both logged near identical daily attack volumes as of August 8, with Georgia at 2,600 attempts per 24 hours and Kazakhstan at 2,652. Both carry nearly the same GreyNoise toolkit tags, including Cisco ASA Scanner, Cisco SSL VPN Bruteforcer, Ivanti Connect Secure Scanner, Pulse Secure VPN Scanner, Palo Alto PAN OS CVE-2020-2034 Crawler, ConnectWise ScreenConnect Auth Bypass Check, and CrushFTP Scanner. Both netblocks were also re announced within the same window in April 2026. A Moldova thread runs through both records as well. Kazakhstan's official RIPE abuse contact lists its address simply as Moldova, and Georgia's uses the same Romanian and Moldovan style street naming convention.

Combined, these two clusters account for roughly 69,955 of the week's 109,000 Ciscoasa hits, or about 64% of the entire spike. What started as an effort to verify that a spike was not a logging bug ended with a much stronger finding, that these two clusters are very likely related infrastructure operating behind different country tagged identities, even though the exact relationship between whoever controls each netblock is not yet settled.

**Methodology note.** OTX AlienVault's ASN field is wrong for both IPs in this pair. It lists AS49393, Solutions Factory Ltd, for the Kazakhstan IP and AS62240, Clouvider Limited, for the Georgia IP, and neither matches the RIPE confirmed AS205997 or AS211486. This is likely stale data lagging the April 2026 route re announcements, and it is a reminder not to trust OTX's ASN field on any recently re routed netblock.

**Assessment. Confidence: High** that the two clusters share infrastructure or show operational linkage, based on the shared domain identifier plus multiple independently corroborating timeline and volume matches. **Confidence: Medium** that they are controlled by the same operator, since shared infrastructure could also mean shared hosting customers, a shared toolkit vendor, or infrastructure that changed hands between actors, rather than one person running both. **Confidence: Low** on the specific individual or individuals behind either cluster, given how much the registrant names and geolocation attribution disagree across sources.

## Investigation 4: Dedicated Scanner or Compromised Host (China)

**222.128.21.18 (China Unicom Beijing Province Network, AS4808).**

This actor is a useful contrast to close out the roster. It is a single-service, single-port actor, with 100% of its traffic hitting Cowrie and exclusively targeting port 22 for SSH, with no lateral spread into other honeypots. This is a clean, repetitive credential-guessing operation rather than the opportunistic multi protocol scanning seen in Investigations 1 through 3.

![WTIR-08 China Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-08/WTIR-08%20China%20Dashboard.png)

What sets this actor apart is the infrastructure underneath it. Every other actor this week runs on a small, dedicated netblock set up specifically for scanning, often abuse-tolerant hosting. This IP instead sits inside a massive, ordinary ISP allocation, 222.128.0.0/14, which has been allocated to China Unicom Beijing since 2008 and serves regular residential and business customers. RIPE Atlas strongly supports the Beijing and China network attribution. Probes registered under AS4808 sit exactly where expected, across Beijing, including the Datun and Haidian districts, and Shanghai, with no conflict at all.

GreyNoise classifies this IP as suspicious rather than malicious, which is a step down in severity from every other actor profiled this week. The tags are exclusively SSH related, including SSH Bruteforcer, SSH Connection Attempt, SSH Alternative Port Crawler, and Web Crawler, with no enterprise VPN or firewall toolkit signature present. AbuseIPDB shows a shorter history too, with 256 reports from 124 distinct sources first reported on March 7, 2026, which is about five months compared to multi year records for every other actor this week. Cisco Talos previously blocklisted this IP, but that listing status now reads EXPIRED, unlike Alsycon's still active entry. VirusTotal shows 11 of 91 vendors flagging malicious, including several Phishing tags, which is most likely reputation carryover from the broader netblock's history rather than activity this honeynet directly observed, since everything captured here is pure SSH brute forcing.

**Assessment. Confidence: Medium.** The shorter abuse history, lower GreyNoise severity, single protocol focus, and legitimate ISP address space together point toward a compromised residential or business host running automated SSH credential guessing, rather than dedicated scanning infrastructure like Investigations 1 through 3. That said, compromise cannot be confirmed from honeynet telemetry alone. This is a well-supported inference, not a device-level forensic finding, which is why it sits at Medium rather than High.

## What This Week Taught Me

Extending spike verification to Ciscoasa paid off immediately. ConPot is still the only honeypot in this series that has actually turned out to be inflating its numbers, but that experience is what taught me not to take any sudden spike at face value, whether it turns out to be real or not. Without running the same check on Ciscoasa, I would have either reported the 109,000 figure without questioning it, or noted the jump and moved on without asking why it happened. Verifying it first is what turned a routine data quality step into this report's strongest finding.

I also learned that GeoIP and even registration record country fields are not reliable enough on their own to decide whether two actors are related. The Georgia and Kazakhstan clusters looked like two unrelated, unfamiliar country tagged actors right up until I checked for shared infrastructure identifiers directly. The thing that actually proved the connection was not geography at all. It was a domain name sitting in a GreyNoise field. If anything, the location data made the picture more confusing rather than less.

The clearest lesson is that not every high volume actor deserves the same category of concern. Four of this week's five profiled actors are running rented, dedicated scanning infrastructure with multi year abuse histories behind them. The China actor's shorter history and lower severity classification point to something closer to a hijacked consumer device. Treating both the same way would have flattened a distinction that actually matters for how you would respond to each.

There are a few things worth checking next week. I want to see whether `zerolimit-servers.cool` resurfaces under new IPs, whether Sentrypeer's collapse from 183,000 to 18,000 was a one off or the start of a trend, and whether the LATAM phishing lead on Alsycon's netblock deserves its own dedicated investigation.
