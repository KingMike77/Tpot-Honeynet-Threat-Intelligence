## Executive Summary

> **At a Glance**
> - **Total events:** 701,000, down about 18% from last week's 854,000
> - **Top ASN, series record:** AS213474 (HomeLine Broadband LLC), 233,529 hits, the largest single-ASN total this series has recorded
> - **Flagship finding:** the infrastructure identity behind the zerolimit-servers.cool family, registered under the name "Vlad Cojuhari," has surfaced under its own ASN (AS205997) for the first time, after two reports of only being reachable through a shared abuse-contact domain
> - **Recurring actor, new behavior:** AS48090 (Techoff Srv Limited), first profiled in WTIR-06 for Solana credential stuffing, returned this week hunting exposed config files and secrets instead
> - **Recurring actor, quiet week:** AS47890 (UNMANAGED LTD), fourth consecutive week in the series, dropped from last week's record 163,513 hits to just 10,712
> - **New actor:** 197.44.53.74 (TE Data, Egypt), a straightforward VNC brute-force source with no attribution puzzle attached

Total traffic came down significantly from last week's record, but the drop is not evenly distributed. Ciscoasa held almost exactly steady in raw volume while every other honeypot's overall footprint shrank, which is why Ciscoasa's share of total traffic actually climbed even as the grand total fell. The reason sits in this week's flagship finding.

WTIR-08 closed with two Ciscoasa-heavy clusters, tagged Georgia and Kazakhstan, tied together by a shared abuse-contact domain, `zerolimit-servers.cool`. WTIR-09 found a third cluster under that same domain, spread across six country attributions. This week, the infrastructure identity behind that domain stopped hiding behind shared registration details and showed up directly: the Kazakhstan-geolocated IP is registered to AS205997, whose RIPE record lists "Vlad Cojuhari" as the AS name and is maintained by `zerolimit-mnt`, the same maintainer handle tied to the domain all along. Three reports in, the registration identity I could only ever describe through someone else's abuse contact now has its own AS number attached to its own name. That's an infrastructure-attribution development, not proof of who is actually operating it, a distinction the investigation below and the confidence tiers throughout this report try to hold onto.

Two established recurring actors had very different weeks. UNMANAGED LTD, which set a series volume record last week, all but disappeared this week and changed its behavior when it did show up. Techoff Srv Limited, last seen four months ago running a Solana-validator credential-stuffing campaign, returned on the same infrastructure but with an entirely different objective.

## What I Changed This Week

Before writing a single line of this report, I ran a volume verification pass on every actor that was going to anchor an investigation, the same way I verified ConPot back in WTIR-06. For each of the four candidate actors I compared **raw record count against unique count of `@timestamp`** in Lens, scoped correctly by honeypot type, before treating any number as final.

Three actors came back clean on the first pass. The fourth, Techoff, initially looked inflated: an unscoped query returned 35,336 records against only 21,264 unique timestamps, a 40% collision rate. Breaking that number down by `type.keyword` showed why: the query had been counting **Suricata IDS alerts and P0f fingerprint entries** alongside the actual honeypot session logs. Those aren't independent attacker interactions, they're additional log lines generated by the same underlying connections. Once scoped to only the honeypots that log actual sessions (Tanner, H0neytr4p, Cowrie), the number dropped to 30,448, four records above the original dashboard's 30,444 tile, a gap small enough to be a refresh-timing artifact rather than a real discrepancy.

That still left one loose end: H0neytr4p alone showed a 15,221-record, 1,847-unique-timestamp split, an 88% collision rate, on its own. Pulling the raw Discover records for the specific spike hour resolved it. Five records sharing an identical timestamp turned out to be five genuinely distinct requests (`/api/error.save`, `/mail/xampp/info.php`, `/administration/dev/phpinfo.php`, `/api/cmd.js`, and one more), each with its own `_id`. H0neytr4p simply logs timestamps at one-second resolution, so a fast scripted actor can fire several distinct requests inside the same logged second. No duplication, just coarse timestamp granularity colliding with a fast scanner.

This is a different failure mode than the one ConPot produced, and worth keeping as a standing check going forward: **before trusting a raw count from any honeypot that isn't Ciscoasa, Cowrie, or Tanner, confirm whether IDS/fingerprinting layers are riding along in the same query, and don't assume a high timestamp-collision rate means duplication without checking what's actually inside the colliding records.**

## Methodology

Sensor: T-Pot 24.04.1 HIVE, Houston, TX, not publicly exposed beyond the honeynet itself. Dashboard: Elastic/Kibana, reporting window Aug 15, 2026 12:00:00 to Aug 22, 2026 12:00:00 per the platform's own time range (Kibana's configured time zone, not independently confirmed as UTC).

**Confidence tiers**, assigned per finding rather than per IP:

| Tier | Standard |
|---|---|
| High | Corroborated by two or more independent sources (this sensor plus at least one of RIPE/AFRINIC, AbuseIPDB, VirusTotal, GreyNoise, OTX), or directly observed and internally consistent after verification |
| Medium | Observed on this sensor and plausible given the actor's known history, but not independently corroborated |
| Low | Single data point, timing coincidence, or a connection I noticed but can't yet substantiate |

**Terminology and limitations:** "Attacks" and "events" refer to logged honeypot interactions, not confirmed compromises. A honeypot recording a login attempt or an HTTP request does not mean any real system was affected. Suricata alert signatures with an attached CVE ID, especially decades-old ones, are frequently generic pattern matches rather than proof that the specific referenced vulnerability was exploited; I favor citing the signature description over the CVE tag where the two diverge, per the pattern documented in this week's Investigation 2. Geolocation and ASN ownership can be genuinely fragmented across a single IP, as seen with Techoff in WTIR-06, so I treat conflicting attribution sources as a feature of the infrastructure rather than an error to resolve to one answer.

## Investigation 1: The Passport Finally Matches the Face (HomeLine Broadband and Vlad Cojuhari)

**Confidence: High**

The 138.226.239.0/24 block, registered to AS213474 (HomeLine Broadband LLC), returned for a second week and grew into the largest single-ASN total this series has ever recorded: 233,529 hits across three IPs (138.226.239.4 at 91,329; .3 at 84,361; .5 at 56,438), every single one of them hitting Ciscoasa exclusively. All three totals verified clean against raw record counts, and the hourly traffic shape for the largest IP showed genuine bursts and ramps across the week rather than a flat artifact.

Independent OSINT corroborates what the volume alone suggests. HomeLine Broadband is documented elsewhere as a Bulgarian ISP whose address space shows up disproportionately in Cisco ASA honeypot datasets, consistent with compromised residential routers or IoT devices being drafted into a botnet and used as proxies, the traffic riding through infected consumer hardware rather than a deliberately rented attack box.

The more interesting development sits with a smaller, related actor. 185.136.15.2, geolocated to Kazakhstan and responsible for 25,640 hits, all against Ciscoasa, resolves to **AS205997**, whose RIPE `aut-num` record lists the AS name as `Vlad_Cojuhari` and is maintained by `zerolimit-mnt`. That is the same maintainer handle behind `zerolimit-servers.cool`, the abuse-contact domain that tied together WTIR-08's Georgia and Kazakhstan clusters and WTIR-09's six-country cluster under AS213474. In both of those prior reports, "Vlad Cojuhari" was a name I only ever reached indirectly, through a shared domain or a disputed registrant field on someone else's ASN. This week, for the first time, that name has its own AS number, its own IP, and its own clean, verified hit count. The traffic for this IP was tightly concentrated in roughly a 24-hour window on August 16 and 17, rather than spread across the full week, a genuine burst rather than sustained activity.

Whether AS213474 and AS205997 are the same operator running two ASNs, or two separate customers of the same underlying infrastructure provider, I can't say definitively. What I can say is that the `zerolimit-mnt` maintainer handle now connects three consecutive weeks of Ciscoasa-heavy activity across at least four ASNs and six country attributions, and that this week is the first time the name behind it was directly attached to the traffic rather than inferred.

## Investigation 2: From Validators to Config Files (Techoff Srv Limited)

**Confidence: High**

AS48090, Techoff Srv Limited, is not a new actor. WTIR-06 profiled this ASN running a Solana-validator credential-stuffing campaign against Cowrie, complete with the "Registration says Andorra, packets say Amsterdam" fragmented-attribution story: RIPE lists the inetnum's country as Andorra, the org record lists a London address, the maintainer is a Bulgarian carrier, and the traffic itself physically routes through Amsterdam.

That same ASN is back this week, and one of its two active IPs, 195.178.110.28, is the exact IP profiled in WTIR-06, contributing 8,336 hits per the original Top 10 Source IP panel. The second, 45.148.10.183, is new to the series and is the one this report's verification pass focused on: 30,448 hits, split across Tanner (15,223), H0neytr4p (15,221), and a handful on Cowrie (4). The 195.178.110.28 figure wasn't independently re-verified with the same count-versus-unique-timestamp method, so I'm treating it as medium confidence pending that check; together, the two IPs put this actor's total footprint at roughly 38,784 for the week.

The objective has changed. There's no Solana-pattern credential stuffing this week. Instead, the Suricata Alert Signature breakdown for 45.148.10.183 shows a shotgun sweep for accidentally exposed secrets and source code:

| Signature | Count | What it's looking for |
|---|---|---|
| ET WEB_SERVER WEB-PHP phpinfo access | 382 | Exposed `phpinfo()` pages leaking server config and paths |
| ET INFO Request to Hidden Environment File - Inbound | 201 | Exposed `.env` files, commonly leaking database and API credentials |
| ET WEB_SERVER Tilde in URI - potential .php~/.inc/.conf source disclosure | 34 combined | Editor-generated backup files exposing source code |
| ET INFO Request for Visual Studio Code sftp.json | 10 | Exposed VS Code SFTP config, frequently containing plaintext credentials |
| GPL WEB_SERVER global.asa access | 3 | The ASP equivalent of the same idea |

Raw Discover records confirm this is genuine automated scanning rather than one-off noise: within a single logged second, this IP fired distinct requests against `/administration/dev/phpinfo.php`, `/mail/xampp/info.php`, `/api/cmd.js`, and `/api/error.save`, each a separate request with its own record ID. This is a fast, scripted actor working down a checklist of common credential-leak paths, not a slow manual probe.

One CVE ID initially attached to this activity, CVE-2002-1149, is worth flagging as almost certainly a red herring: it's a 20-plus-year-old, medium-severity information-disclosure CVE that the ET ruleset has loosely tagged onto the generic phpinfo signature. I'm treating the signature description as the meaningful evidence here, not the attached CVE.

## Investigation 3: The Regular Has a Quiet Week (UNMANAGED LTD)

**Confidence: Medium**

AS47890, Romania, first profiled in WTIR-07, has now appeared in four consecutive reports. Last week it posted this series' largest single-actor total, 163,513 hits. This week, on a new IP (2.57.121.120, replacing 193.46.255.112), it posted just 10,712, a roughly 93% drop.

The behavior changed along with the volume. Previous weeks documented targeted CVE-2018-13379 Fortinet SSL VPN exploitation attempts and Asterisk/FreePBX enumeration. This week's traffic instead spread thinly across a set of non-standard high ports, 8000, 8002, 8011, 8019, and 3443, almost entirely caught by Honeytrap, the honeypot's low-interaction catch-all for connections that don't match a more specific emulated service. Secondary honeypots picked up only trace amounts: Dionaea (62), Sentrypeer (40), Tanner (34), H0neytr4p (33), Ciscoasa (25).

![WTIR-10 Romanian Threat Actor Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-10/WTIR-10%20Romanian%20Threat%20Actor%20Dashboard.png)

I'm reading this as a broad, low-effort port sweep rather than a targeted exploitation attempt, a meaningfully different posture from the FreePBX and Fortinet-focused activity documented in prior weeks. ## Investigation 4: A New Face, an Old Protocol (Egypt)

**Confidence: High**

197.44.53.74, registered to AS8452 (TE Data, Telecom Egypt's consumer ISP, Giza), is the one genuinely new actor introduced this week, and it's a straightforward one. Verified total: 6,995 hits, entirely against Heralding, entirely on port 5900, VNC's standard port.

![WTIR-10 Egypt Threat Actor Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-10/WTIR-10%20Egypt%20Threat%20Actor%20Dashboard.png)

Two independent fingerprinting sources agree on the same detail without prompting from each other: GreyNoise tags this IP "Looks Like Windows 7 server," and this sensor's own P0f OS distribution panel separately reports Windows 7 or 8. The Suricata signature breakdown supports genuine protocol engagement rather than a simple port knock: 13,992 hits on "GPL INFO VNC server response," meaning real RFB protocol negotiation completed, alongside network-layer noise (broken ACKs, spurious retransmissions) consistent with scripted, automated connection handling. One rarer signature, "SURICATA RFB Unexpected State in Parser," fired once, suggesting a slightly non-standard VNC client implementation.

![WTIR-10 Egypt Suricata Alert Signature](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-10/WTIR-10%20Egypt%20Suricata%20Alert%20Signature.png)

![WTIR-10 Egypt P0f OS Distribution](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-10/WTIR-10%20Egypt%20P0f%20OS%20Distribution.png)

![WTIR-10 Egypt Attack Map and Histograms](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-10/WTIR-10%20Egypt%20Attack%20Map%20and%20Histograms.png)

AbuseIPDB independently corroborates this as an active, ongoing source: 166 reports from 27 distinct sources, 76% abuse confidence, first reported June 2025, with reports as recent as the day this report was written, consistently describing VNC authentication attempts using DES challenge-response. This sensor's own traffic shape matches that description exactly: two sharp bursts, roughly August 16-17 and again August 19-20, consistent with a scripted brute-force tool running for hours, pausing, and resuming, rather than continuous scanning.

The password wordlist is mostly unremarkable (`gandalf`, `monopoly`, `catdog`, `billboy`), but two entries break the pattern: `5Wr2i7H8` looks like a randomly generated string rather than a dictionary word, and `HARLEY` and `SHADOW` are the only fully capitalized entries in an otherwise lowercase list. Worth noting as a minor inconsistency in an otherwise generic wordlist, not a strong signal on its own.

![WTIR-10 Egypt Password Tagcloud](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-10/WTIR-10%20Egypt%20Password%20Tagcloud.png)

Unlike every other actor in this report, this one carries no attribution puzzle. TE Data's registration is a single, clean AFRINIC record with a straightforward abuse contact, and VirusTotal shows zero detections across 91 vendors, likely because VNC brute-forcing against a honeypot doesn't trip the malware or phishing signatures those engines are built to catch. The infrastructure picture, a fixed-line consumer ISP rather than a hosting provider, is consistent with a compromised residential endpoint rather than deliberately provisioned attacker infrastructure, a different profile from every other actor covered this week.

## What This Week Taught Me

Not every inflated-looking number is a duplication bug. ConPot's issue in WTIR-06 was genuinely duplicate log ingestion. Techoff's apparent inflation this week was a completely different mechanism, IDS alerts and OS-fingerprint metadata being counted as if they were independent attacker interactions, when they're actually secondary log lines generated by the same underlying sessions. Both produce the same symptom, a raw count that looks too high, but they need different fixes: ConPot needed deduplication, Techoff just needed correct scoping. I'm adding "check for non-session honeypot types riding along in the query" as a standing step before I trust any total, right alongside the timestamp-collision check.

The other lesson is about patience with attribution. Three reports ago, "Vlad Cojuhari" was a name buried in someone else's RIPE record. This week it has its own ASN. I don't think that would have surfaced if I'd stopped tracking the zerolimit domain after WTIR-09 just because that report already had a strong finding. Some threads take three weeks to pay off.

## What Remains Unconfirmed

- **Whether AS213474 and AS205997 are the same operator or separate customers of shared infrastructure.** The maintainer handle and behavioral pattern connect them; nothing yet proves common ownership beyond that.
- **Whether this week's Ciscoasa activity is connected to CVE-2026-20349**, the Cisco ASA/FTD Remote Access SSL VPN denial-of-service flaw published August 11, 2026 per NVD and added to CISA's KEV catalog the same day. The timing is suggestive, but I don't have a Suricata signature hit tied to that specific CVE ID this week, and I'm not asserting a connection on timing alone.
- **What caused UNMANAGED LTD's shift from targeted exploitation to broad port scanning.** Could be the same operator testing new targets, a pause in a specific campaign, or something else entirely.
- **Whether HomeLine Broadband's traffic is genuinely botnet-proxied residential/IoT devices**, as external sources suggest, or something else specific to this sensor's exposure. I have no direct evidence from this honeynet either way beyond the ASN's general reputation.
