# Weekly Threat Intelligence Report — WTIR-05-2026

**Reporting Period:** July 11–18, 2026
**Author:** Michael Mensah

---

## Executive Summary

The honeynet logged 933,000 attack events this week, a 52% increase over last week's 613,000.

Four actors stood out enough to profile in depth. The first, tied to Psychz Networks, is a returning VoIP toll-fraud operation caught live via Shodan running spoofed SIP calls against a UK phone number through Korean and Chinese carrier networks.

The second and third are a pair of DigitalOcean-hosted IPs that share an identical software fingerprint and are most likely the same VPN provider's exit nodes, relaying scan traffic from their own customer base rather than attacking directly.

The fourth, and the most concerning of the week, is a host registered through a Seychelles-based reseller but physically sitting in the Netherlands, actively scanning for exploitable enterprise VPN and firewall appliances (Ivanti, Pulse Secure, F5 BIG-IP, Fortinet, and Palo Alto), the kind of access point that regularly precedes real ransomware intrusions.

ConPot, my ICS/SCADA honeypot, also cracked the top five most-hit honeypots this week at roughly three times its usual share of traffic, suggesting a dedicated industrial-control-system scanning campaign ran alongside the usual web and VoIP activity.

---

## Tools I used for this report

Along with my T-Pot/Kibana dashboard for the raw honeynet telemetry, I ran the interesting IPs from this week through my usual OSINT stack: Hurricane Electric's BGP Toolkit (bgp.he.net) for netblock and ASN routing data as well as the ARIN and AFRINIC RDAP and WHOIS records tracing ownership and registration chains, Shodan for host fingerprinting and open ports, VirusTotal for vendor detection consensus, AbuseIPDB for abuse confidence scores and report history, GreyNoise for scan classification and behavior tags, Cisco Talos for reputation and content categorization, and OTX AlienVault for community pulses and passive DNS. I also used RIPE Atlas probes where it helped confirm a host's actual routing path and location.

## Why Seychelles, of all places

I'll be honest, I had to look this one up. Seychelles is a tiny island nation off the east coast of Africa, way out in the Indian Ocean, northeast of Madagascar. We're talking about 115 islands and a total population of around 120,000 people, which is smaller than plenty of individual zip codes here in the US. Here's roughly where it sits:

![Seychelles Locator Map](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-05/WTIR-05%20Seychelles%20Locator%20Map.png)

It's just not a place that shows up on "top attacking countries" lists. That list is usually the same handful of countries every time, like the US, China, Russia, Netherlands, and Germany, places with huge hosting infrastructure and millions of internet-connected devices. So seeing a country with almost no digital footprint land in a top-five spot for my honeynet was exactly the kind of thing that makes me stop and dig instead of just noting it and moving on.

### So who is Alsycon, really?

Here's where it got genuinely interesting. "Alsycon B.V." doesn't sound Seychellois at all, and it isn't. Once I started tracing the registration records, I found three layers stacked on top of each other.

First, the IP block itself is announced by Alsycon B.V., a Dutch company that runs VPS, dedicated server, and colocation hosting out of Wormer, Netherlands. Second, Alsycon is actually leasing that IP space from a larger block owned by a company called HostUS Solutions LLC, and HostUS is legally registered in Mahé, Seychelles, with an address at the Providence Industrial Estate and admin contacts split between Seychelles and London. Third, when I checked GreyNoise for the server's actual physical location, it placed the machine in Lelystad, Netherlands, which was confirmed by the reverse DNS record and routing data.

So the Seychelles tag on my dashboard wasn't really wrong, it just wasn't telling the whole story. It traces back to where the parent hosting company is legally incorporated, not to where the server physically sits. And just to make things even messier, OTX's own database geolocates this same exact IP to South Africa. So depending on which tool I ask, I get a different country every time. That mismatch alone made this worth writing up.

### What this box is actually doing

Once I had the IP isolated, I ran it through my usual research stack, and the more I found, the more concerning it got.

Shodan showed this server running OpenSSH 7.4, a version from around 2016. That's old enough to be carrying a long list of unpatched vulnerabilities, including a 9.8-severity remote code execution bug from 2023 and the "Terrapin" SSH downgrade attack. That tells me this is very likely a neglected or compromised machine, not something anyone's actively maintaining.

GreyNoise is really what changed how seriously I took this one. It flagged the IP as suspicious and malicious, and it listed out exactly what it's been caught scanning for, and none of it is random.

![Alsycon GreyNoise Tags](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-05/WTIR-05%20Alsycon%20GreyNoise%20Tags.png)

This host has been actively probing for Ivanti Connect Secure and Ivanti VTM, Pulse Secure VPN, F5 BIG-IP, Sophos XG Firewall's login portal, Palo Alto's PAN-OS, and a known Fortinet credential-disclosure bug. It's also been checking for a ConnectWise ScreenConnect auth-bypass, on top of WordPress enumeration, Outlook Web Access crawling, FreePBX probing, and a few web app exploit attempts.

Nearly every one of those VPN and firewall products has a real history of being the way ransomware groups get their initial foothold into a company's network. Seeing all of them scanned together doesn't look like generic internet background noise anymore. It looks like something specifically hunting for a way into corporate networks.

AbuseIPDB backed all of this up in a big way. It showed 100% abuse confidence, with 673 reports from 318 different sources, by far the widest spread of independent reporters I found on anything this week. Honeypots in Canada, France, the US, Germany, Australia, and Brazil have all logged this same IP doing port scans, PBX probing, Telnet and SSH brute-forcing, FTP attempts, and even Tomcat exploitation attempts. One reporter in Germany running a Citrix honeypot logged the exact same "Seychelles" country tag I did, so this labeling quirk isn't unique to my setup.

VirusTotal had 11 out of 91 vendors flagging it malicious, with a couple specifically tagging it for phishing.

![Alsycon VirusTotal](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-05/WTIR-05%20Alsycon%20VirusTotal.png)

And OTX turned up 15 different pulses referencing this IP from honeypot networks as far apart as Australia, several European countries, and Turkey, confirming it's also been caught running SMB and database attacks, Android botnet activity, and Redis database exploitation, not just the web scanning I saw myself.

One last thing worth mentioning is that this IP failed 2 of 83 blocklist checks, specifically the two that flag compromised machines and open proxies actively being used for abuse. That fits with everything else I found.

So here's where I landed on this one. It's an old, unpatched server sitting physically in the Netherlands, running on IP space leased from a Seychelles-registered hosting company, and it's actively scanning the internet for exploitable enterprise VPN and firewall products, exactly the kind of access point that keeps showing up in real ransomware attacks.

## The other three actors from this week

**108.181.56.189** turned out to be a returning face. It belongs to Psychz Networks, the same hosting provider behind the SIP scanning campaign I wrote about back in WTIR-03, and with 171,656 hits it was the single biggest source of traffic this week. I got lucky here, because Shodan had actually captured this IP's traffic live.

![Psychz Shodan SIP Capture](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-05/WTIR-05%20Psychz%20Shodan%20SIP%20Capture.png)

It shows the server sending real SIP call requests through both Korea Telecom and China Unicom's networks, both aimed at the exact same UK phone number, using a different spoofed caller name each time. That's not random scanning. That's someone deliberately testing toll fraud or caller ID spoofing against one specific target.

![Psychz AbuseIPDB](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-05/WTIR-05%20Psychz%20AbuseIPDB.png)

AbuseIPDB shows it's still very active, and the reports show it cycling through dozens of spoofed device signatures, including Cisco phones, Yealink, Grandstream, Mitel, and Asterisk, basically testing which kinds of phone systems will respond to it.

**164.92.93.36** and **64.23.175.24** showed up separately in my top 10, but once I compared them side by side, I became convinced they're actually the same operation. Both are hosted on DigitalOcean, both run the exact same OpenSSH version and configuration down to the algorithm ordering, and both got flagged by Talos as "Personal VPN" services. My best read is that this is one VPN provider running at least two exit nodes, and what I'm seeing is scan traffic coming from that VPN's own customers passing through it, not the provider attacking anyone directly. Both boxes are well-patched and current, which is a pretty big contrast to the Netherlands box above. This looks like a legitimately-run service that's just being used, or misused, by whoever happens to be connected to it at the time.

The Seychelles thread ended up being the most worthwhile thing I did this week, and not because of the country itself. It was a good reminder that when something in your data looks statistically strange, it's usually worth pulling on. What started as a labeling oddity turned into the most concerning actor of the four, an old, vulnerable machine actively hunting for exploitable enterprise VPN and firewall appliances, which is exactly the kind of foothold that shows up again and again in real ransomware cases.

It also taught me to be a little more careful going forward. That same IP gave me four different countries depending on which tool I asked. It was Seychelles by legal registration, Netherlands by physical hosting, and South Africa according to OTX. None of those were wrong exactly. They were just answering slightly different questions. It's worth keeping in mind the next time a geolocation field looks like it's giving me the final answer.
