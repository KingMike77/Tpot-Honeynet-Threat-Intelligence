# WTIR-03-2026 — T-Pot Honeynet Threat Intelligence Report

**Reporting Period:** June 20 – June 27, 2026
**Published:** June 28, 2026
**Author:** Michael Mensah

---

## Executive Summary

During the week of June 20–27, the T-Pot honeynet recorded approximately 1.27 million attack attempts. Nearly 75% targeted SIP-based VoIP infrastructure, while sustained scanning against VNC services revealed continued exploitation of legacy remote access software, including active attempts against CVE-2006-2369. Three recurring threat actors accounted for a significant portion of observed activity, highlighting the persistence of internet-wide scanning campaigns against exposed services.

---

## Methodology

This report is based on telemetry collected by a public-facing T-Pot honeynet deployed over a seven-day period. Data sources include Elastic Stack dashboards, Suricata IDS alerts, T-Pot honeypot logs, and external enrichment from AbuseIPDB, GreyNoise, Maltiverse, SpiderFoot, and ASN lookups. Metrics reflect interactions with intentionally exposed decoy services and should be interpreted as observed attack activity against the honeynet rather than global internet trends.

---

## Overview

This week my T-Pot honeypot recorded approximately 1.27 million attack attempts over 7 days. A honeypot is a system deliberately set up to attract attackers — it looks like a real server but exists only to observe and log what attackers do when they find it.

This week had one clear theme: **remote access tools are under heavy attack.** Two specific technologies — VoIP phone systems (SIP) and remote desktop software (VNC) — made up the majority of all attack traffic. Three out of the top five attacking IPs were going after one of those two things exclusively.

## What Attackers Were Trying to Log In With

The most common usernames were **root** and **admin** as expected, but several entries in the tagcloud tell a more specific story.

**Solana is still being targeted.** The usernames solana, sol, and solv appeared in the username tagcloud this week — and the same three words appeared in the password tagcloud as well. In WTIR-02 we noted a credential dictionary specifically built for Solana blockchain validator infrastructure. That dictionary is still in active circulation. Whoever built it is still running it.

**CI/CD and cloud infrastructure is being targeted by name.** Usernames like cloud deployer, devops, deploy, and runner suggest attackers are building credential lists that specifically target automation accounts — the service accounts used by deployment pipelines, build systems, and cloud orchestration tools. These accounts often have elevated permissions and are less likely to have MFA enabled.

**Gaming servers are in the mix.** Minecraft, teamspeak, and steam all appeared as usernames, pointing to credential lists targeting gaming communities running self-hosted servers — a segment that often runs on residential or low-cost hosting with minimal security configuration.

**Frappe and Odoo targeting.** Frappe (the framework behind ERPNext) and Odoo both appeared — one as a username, one as a password. Both are open source business management platforms commonly self-hosted by small businesses. Attackers building credential lists for specific software platforms are doing reconnaissance, not just spraying generic passwords.

**Password complexity substitutions are everywhere.** The password tagcloud showed four variations of the same word: P@sswOrd, PasswOrd, p@sswOrd, and passwOrd. This tells us attackers know that many systems enforce basic complexity rules — at least one uppercase letter, at least one number or symbol — and they are pre-building variations that satisfy those rules. If your password policy only requires complexity without enforcing length or blocking common patterns, these dictionaries will satisfy it.

The keyboard walk patterns from WTIR-02 continued to appear: 1qaz@WSX, 1q2w3e4r, and 1qaz2wsx. The word toor — root spelled backwards — also appeared, a classic Linux default that still gets tried because it still occasionally works.

## The Three Actors We Looked Into

### Actor 1 — 87.106.117.109 | Germany | VoIP Mass Scanner Hitting SentryPeer

**555,031 hits | #1 attacker this week**

![Kibana filtered to 87.106.117.109](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-03/WITR-03%20Kibana%20filtered%20to%2087.106.117.109.png)

This single IP was responsible for 43% of all attack traffic this week. As the Kibana screenshot shows, every single one of those 555,031 hits went to **SentryPeer** — the honeypot that pretends to be a business VoIP phone system.

**What is SentryPeer and why does it matter?**

SentryPeer emulates a SIP (Session Initiation Protocol) server — the technology that most business phone systems use to make and receive calls. When an attacker hits SentryPeer, they are probing for a real phone system they can exploit. The goal is typically **toll fraud**: gaining unauthorized access to a company's phone system and using it to make calls that get billed to the victim. Toll fraud costs businesses an estimated $1 billion or more annually worldwide. An attacker who successfully accesses a phone system can rack up thousands of dollars in international call charges overnight before anyone notices.

The fact that this IP sent every single hit exclusively to SentryPeer — not to SSH, not to web servers, not to anything else — tells us this is purpose-built tooling for VoIP attacks, not a general scanner that happened to hit a phone system along the way.

**Who is behind it:**
The IP is hosted by IONOS SE (a major German web hosting company, formerly 1&1) in Berlin under AS8560. The entire surrounding subnet was flagged as malicious by multiple sources, suggesting coordinated infrastructure rather than a single compromised machine.

**What stood out:**
A German network administrator reported that this IP was using the User-Agent string **Avaya One-X Deskphone Edition 6.2.2** in its SIP requests — impersonating a legitimate enterprise desk phone model. This is a deliberate attempt to blend in with normal office equipment and potentially bypass security filters. The customized Avaya One-X Deskphone Edition 6.2.2 User-Agent suggests the operator intentionally configured the scanner to resemble legitimate enterprise VoIP equipment.

The IP also had **port 3389 (Remote Desktop Protocol) open and exposed to the internet**, meaning whoever operates this machine keeps remote access to it. That is consistent with maintained attack infrastructure, not a compromised machine running on autopilot.

### Actor 2 — 143.198.239.107 | United States | Five-Year VNC Scanner Hitting Honeytrap

**33,762 hits | #5 attacker this week**

![Kibana filtered to 143.198.239.107](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-03/WITR-03%20Kibana%20filtered%20to%20143.198.239.107.png)

The Kibana screenshot tells the story immediately. Every single one of this IP's 33,762 hits went to **Honeytrap** on **port 5901** — one of the two VNC remote desktop ports. This IP was not doing broad scanning. It was looking specifically for VNC servers.

**What is Honeytrap and what does port 5901 tell us?**

Honeytrap is a generic port listener — it opens up on hundreds of ports and waits to see what connects. When Honeytrap sees traffic exclusively on port 5901, that means the attacker already knew what port they wanted before they got there. Port 5901 is a VNC display port (specifically display :1). VNC is a remote desktop tool that lets someone take control of another computer over the internet — the same concept as Remote Desktop on Windows but available on all operating systems.

An attacker who successfully authenticates to an exposed VNC server gets full visual control of that computer: they see the screen, control the mouse and keyboard, and can do anything the logged-in user can do. For an unattended server, that means complete takeover with no further exploitation needed.

**What stood out — the five-year history:**

The numbers for this IP on AbuseIPDB are extraordinary. It has been reported **8,541 times by 47 distinct sources** with a **100% abuse confidence score** — the maximum possible rating. The first report was filed on **March 24, 2021**. That means this IP has been continuously running malicious VNC scanning for over five years without DigitalOcean suspending or blocking it.

Every recent report targets port 5900 or 5901 — pure VNC enumeration, consistent across the entire five-year history. Automated monitoring services like ScanThe.Net filed over a dozen reports in a single 24-hour window during this reporting period alone.

### Actor 3 — VNC Exploitation Campaign | A 20-Year-Old Vulnerability Still Being Exploited in 2026

This week's third profile is not a single IP. It is a pattern visible across the Suricata intrusion detection data — one that shows how long vulnerabilities can survive on the internet when patching does not happen.

![Suricata CVE Table](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-03/WITR-03%20Suricata%20CVE%20table.png)

**What Suricata detected this week:**

| Suricata Alert | Count |
|---|---|
| GPL INFO VNC server response | 763,665 |
| ET EXPLOIT VNC Server Not Requiring Authentication | 16,470 |
| ET INFO VNC Authentication Failure | 16,467 |
| CVE-2006-2369 exploit attempts | 16,481 |

The top Suricata signature for the entire week — by a massive margin — was VNC server response with 763,665 detections. To understand what that means: this signature fires when a VNC server sends its initial handshake response to an incoming connection. 763,665 times this week, our honeypot was responding to VNC connection attempts from scanners actively probing for remote desktop access.

**What is CVE-2006-2369?**

This vulnerability was discovered in **2006** — nearly 20 years ago. It affects RealVNC versions 4.1.0 and 4.1.1, and also appeared in products that embedded the same code, including certain Cisco CallManager versions.

During the VNC handshake, the server advertises supported authentication methods. RealVNC 4.1.0 and 4.1.1 failed to verify that the client selected one of those advertised methods. An attacker could simply request "no authentication," and the server would grant full remote desktop access without validating any credentials.

Metasploit has had a working exploit module for this since at least 2011. Nmap includes a built-in script called **realvnc-auth-bypass** that checks for it automatically. Exploiting this requires no technical skill — the tools have been publicly available for over a decade.

The 16,481 Suricata alerts for CVE-2006-2369 in a single week do not mean new vulnerable software is being installed. They mean old unpatched software is still reachable on the internet, and automated scanning frameworks probe for it continuously because it still works.

Both Actor 1 and Actor 2 contributed to the VNC traffic this week. This is not a single actor running a single campaign — it is multiple operators targeting the same attack surface simultaneously because the tooling is mature, the targets are still out there, and it still works.

**What this means practically:**

VNC should not be directly exposed to the internet. If you need remote desktop access to a system, put it behind a VPN, use strong authentication, and run it on a non-standard port. Any system running RealVNC 4.1.1 or earlier that is reachable from the internet without additional controls is at serious risk of complete takeover with no password required.

## Key Takeaways

**VoIP systems are being hunted at industrial scale.** One IP sent 555,031 SIP requests in a single week while spoofing legitimate enterprise phone hardware. Toll fraud is the goal — attackers who find an accessible phone system can generate thousands of dollars in unauthorized calls before anyone notices. SIP services should never be exposed to the internet without strong authentication and IP allowlisting.

**VNC is under coordinated attack from multiple actors simultaneously.** Three separate data points converged this week: 763,665 VNC connection attempts from the Suricata data, a dedicated five-year VNC scanning platform in the top attackers list, and 16,481 active exploit attempts against a 20-year-old authentication bypass vulnerability. Any VNC service exposed to the internet without strong controls should be treated as a priority fix.

**Returning actors keep returning.** 108.181.63.2 and 5.83.143.40 both appeared in WTIR-02 and both are back this week. Infrastructure that survives across multiple reporting periods without takedown is maintained infrastructure — someone is actively running it. Blocklisting and abuse reports alone are not stopping these campaigns.

**Old vulnerabilities do not retire when patches come out.** CVE-2006-2369 is nearly 20 years old and generating thousands of active exploit attempts every week. Patch management is not a task you complete once — it is ongoing, and the backlog of unpatched systems on the internet means even ancient vulnerabilities remain profitable targets.
