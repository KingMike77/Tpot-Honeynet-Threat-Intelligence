## Executive Summary

This week I tried a new way of picking which attackers to investigate. It worked, and it also caught me making a mistake I had probably been making for six weeks.

The short version is this. The sensor recorded about 712,000 real attack events between July 18 and July 25. The dashboard first showed 901,000. The gap is explained below, and it turned out to be my error rather than a broken system.

Three attackers were investigated in depth. One was hunting cryptocurrency servers. One was casing phone systems from a rented Windows machine. One was running phone bill fraud against a single UK number for the third time in six weeks.

The biggest lesson had nothing to do with attackers. One dashboard panel was labeled Attacks while it actually counted log records, and for one honeypot those two things differ enormously. A single incoming packet had produced 42,833 log entries and turned an innocent server into my top ranked threat actor.

No software failed. The honeypot logged correctly and the pipeline indexed correctly. The panel label was misleading, and I trusted it without checking. I have since built a corrected dashboard that counts what its labels promise, and added a data verification step to the weekly process that runs before any analysis begins.

## How to Read This Report

This report is written for two audiences at once.

If you are not in cybersecurity, read the four investigations straight through. They are written as stories, and every technical term is explained where it first appears. You can skip anything labeled as technical detail without losing the thread.

If you work in security, the investigations carry the reasoning and the reference sections at the end carry the operational content. Confidence levels and supporting evidence are stated for every assessment, and the gaps in my knowledge are listed explicitly.

Three terms come up constantly, so it is worth defining them here.

An **IP address** is the numeric address of a machine on the internet, written like 195.178.110.30. It works roughly like a street address for a computer.

A **port** is a numbered door on that machine. Different services listen on different doors. Web pages arrive on port 80, email on port 25, and remote logins on port 22. Knowing which door an attacker knocked on tells you what they were looking for.

An **AS number**, written like AS48090, identifies the company that operates a block of internet addresses. It tells you which provider is hosting the attacker.

## Methodology

I follow the same process every week.

1. I look at the overall attack numbers across all honeypots.
2. I pick which honeypots and which attackers to investigate.
3. I check that the numbers mean what I think they mean before analyzing anything. This step is new as of this week, and Investigation 4 explains why it was added.
4. I research each attacker using outside sources. These include the Hurricane Electric BGP Toolkit, Shodan, VirusTotal, AbuseIPDB, GreyNoise, Cisco Talos, and OTX AlienVault.
5. When sources disagree with each other, I write down the disagreement instead of picking a favorite.
6. I compare findings against earlier reports to identify returning attackers.
7. I write up the behavior, map it to MITRE ATT&CK, and pull out detection ideas.

Throughout the report I separate what I observed from what I concluded, and I label how confident I am in each conclusion. Where the evidence runs out, I say so.

## What I Changed This Week

For five weeks I picked the three loudest attackers overall and investigated those. That approach works, but it has a blind spot. Whichever service gets hammered hardest takes over the entire report, and quieter services never get examined.

So this week I flipped the method. I picked the three busiest honeypots first, then investigated the single busiest attacker on each one.

The idea was to find out whether different attackers specialize in different services. They do, and the difference is dramatic. The change also made me look closely at a honeypot I would normally have skimmed past, and that is where the week became interesting.

## Meet the Honeypots

A honeypot is a fake system built to be attacked. It looks like a real server, but nothing valuable sits behind it. Everything that touches it is unwanted by definition, which makes the logs unusually clean because there is no legitimate traffic to filter out.

My sensor runs about twenty of them. Four of them matter this week.

**Cowrie** pretends to be a Linux server with SSH open, which is the remote login service that administrators use to manage machines. Attackers try to guess usernames and passwords. Cowrie records every attempt including the exact credentials tried, which reveals what the attacker was hoping to find.

**Honeytrap** is a catch all sensor. It listens on a wide range of ports and records anything that connects. It captures less detail than the specialized honeypots but covers far more ground, so it catches scanning activity the others would miss.

**Sentrypeer** pretends to be a VoIP phone system, which is the kind of internet based phone service most offices run. Attackers target these systems to make free calls at someone else's expense. Sentrypeer records the phone numbers they try to dial, and that is often the single most revealing piece of evidence.

**ConPot** pretends to be industrial control equipment, meaning the computers that run power grids, water treatment plants, and factory floors. It is the honeypot most relevant to my background in operational technology security, and this week it was the one that fooled me.

## Investigation 1: The Crypto Hunter

**IP address:** 195.178.110.30
**Honeypot:** Cowrie, the fake SSH server
**Events:** 9,825

Each investigation opens with the box below. It states what I concluded, how confident I am, and what that conclusion rests on. Separating the conclusion from the evidence lets you judge the reasoning rather than taking my word for it.

> **Assessment, Confidence High.** This is a password guessing campaign specifically hunting servers that run Solana cryptocurrency software.
> **Evidence.** The credentials it tried, 350,487 abuse reports from 547 independent sources, a GreyNoise classification of malicious with verified traffic, a Cisco Talos blocklist entry, and a single purpose host profile.

Cowrie was the busiest honeypot this week at 219,000 events, and the busiest single attacker against it was a Bulgarian registered address hammering the SSH login port.

![Cowrie Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Cowrie%20Elastic%20Dashboard.png)

![Cowrie Top Attacker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Cowrie%20Top%20Attacker.png)

### What gave it away

Most password guessing attacks are boring. They try usernames like root and admin with passwords like 123456, aimed at anything that answers. This one was different, and the giveaway was sitting right in the credential list.

![Cowrie Usernames](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Cowrie%20Top%20Attacker%20Usernames.png)

![Cowrie Passwords](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Cowrie%20Top%20Attacker%20Passwords.png)

The usernames were solana, sol, solv, sol123, and solv123.

Those are the account names that Solana cryptocurrency servers typically run under. Solana validators and RPC nodes handle real money in the form of staking rewards, transaction processing, and wallet keys. Anyone who logs into one of those machines can steal directly from it.

So this attacker was not spraying the whole internet hoping to get lucky. They were looking for one specific kind of machine, and they knew exactly what account names to try.

### Confirming it was not only me

A single sensor can produce misleading results, so I checked whether anyone else was seeing the same activity.

AbuseIPDB is a database where organizations report attacks they receive. It held 350,487 reports for this address from 547 separate sources going back to August 2024. Reporters in Germany, France, Finland, Canada, and the United States were logging attempts to log in as sol and solana from this exact address. The most recent report was submitted eighteen seconds before I looked.

My honeypot caught 9,825 of those attempts. That works out to roughly three thousandths of one percent of what this operator has been doing. It is a useful reminder of how small a single sensor's view really is.

### Where is it, actually?

I checked seven sources for the location of this address. They returned four different countries.

| Source | Says it is in | Network operator |
|---|---|---|
| My dashboard | Bulgaria | Techoff Srv Limited |
| VirusTotal | Bulgaria | Techoff Srv Limited |
| AbuseIPDB | Amsterdam, Netherlands | Techoff, domain dmzhost.co |
| Cisco Talos | Andorra | Neterra Ltd |
| OTX AlienVault | Virginia, USA | Combahton GmbH |
| Shodan | Lelystad, Netherlands | Techoff Srv Limited |
| GreyNoise | Amsterdam, Netherlands | Techoff Srv Limited |

The registration paperwork explains the confusion. The address block is registered with a country code of Andorra. The company behind it is registered in the UK at a residential street address in North London. The technical contact is a Bulgarian telecom company. The machine itself physically sits in the Netherlands.

None of the sources is wrong. Each one is reading a different piece of the same deliberately confusing record.

There is a pattern here that is worth remembering. The paperwork says Andorra, and the traffic says Amsterdam. Sources that read registration databases tell you what someone typed on a form. Sources that watch actual network traffic tell you where the packets come from. For questions about where an attacker really is, trust the second kind.

### The detail that says the most

Every organization that owns internet addresses is required to publish an abuse contact, which is an email address where people can report attacks coming from their network. This one is a free Gmail account, listed as dmzhostabuse@gmail.com.

A company operating 256 internet addresses, with 350,487 abuse reports against just one of them, lists a personal Gmail account as its official abuse contact. That is not an oversight. A working abuse desk is the minimum expectation for a legitimate hosting provider. A Gmail address can be ignored, abandoned, or never read at all.

The timeline makes the point clearly. The company registered with RIPE in November 2024. It received the address block this attacker uses in January 2025. Cisco Talos added the address to its blocklist under the category of attackers in September 2025. As of July 2026 it is still attacking, with 547 organizations reporting it.

That is ten months on a major vendor's blocklist with no consequence.

**Technical detail on RPKI.** The internet has a system for verifying that a network is authorized to use the addresses it claims. This block passes those checks cleanly. That is worth stating because some security tools treat a clean result as a mild sign of trustworthiness. It is not one. The check confirms the paperwork is in order. It says nothing about what the machine is actually doing.

### This is not one bad machine but a bad block

Cisco Talos rates the reputation of 77 addresses in this same block. Nearly every one of them is rated Poor. This is not a compromised server sitting on an otherwise clean network. The whole block is being used for abuse, which makes blocking the entire range a defensible response rather than a heavy handed one.

### How it behaves

GreyNoise runs sensors worldwide and classifies this address as malicious, tagging it as an SSH brute forcer. It also marks the address as not spoofable, meaning GreyNoise watched it complete real network handshakes. A faked address cannot do that, so the traffic genuinely comes from this machine.

GreyNoise observed it attacking targets in Australia, Canada, Germany, France, Poland, Singapore, and the United States within a two day window. That covers seven countries across four continents.

This explains the strange shape of the attack pattern below.

![Cowrie Actor Histogram](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Cowrie%20Top%20Attacker%20Histogram.png)

The sawtooth shape consists of bursts of 500 to 620 attempts followed by deep drops, repeating over and over. This is not the attacker slowing down. It is the attacker working through a global target list and cycling back to me periodically. The gaps are the periods when they are busy hitting everyone else.

### The machine itself

Shodan scans the internet and records what services each address runs. It shows that this host has exactly one port open, which is SSH. There is no website, no email service, and no management console. There is nothing except the service it uses to attack.

The machine runs OpenSSH 8.2p1 on Ubuntu 20.04, a version that stopped receiving standard support in April 2025. The profile fits a disposable machine spun up from an old template for a single purpose, rather than a legitimate server that was compromised.

Its SSH host key fingerprint is a2:e7:e0:9b:7a:f5:47:eb:23:50:1d:ec:76:36:28:44. These fingerprints are supposed to be unique to each machine, but operators who clone virtual machines often copy the same key across an entire fleet. Given that 77 addresses in the same block carry poor reputations, that fingerprint is worth searching for.

There is one thing I noticed across reports. Shodan places this machine in Lelystad, Netherlands. Last week's most investigated attacker was also physically located in Lelystad under a completely different company name. Lelystad is a known low cost data center town, so this is not necessarily the same operator. Even so, abuse friendly hosting keeps turning up in the same physical place regardless of what the paperwork claims.

## Investigation 2: The Campaign That Only Looked Dead

**IP address:** 192.159.99.105
**Honeypot:** Honeytrap, the catch all listener
**Events:** 27,140

> **Assessment, Confidence High.** This is reconnaissance against phone system administration interfaces, run from a rented Windows machine, which then moved on to active fraud attempts.
> **Evidence.** My own traffic logs, matching daily reports from a German sensor operator, later fraud reports from separate sensors in the United States and Australia, and the machine's own service profile.

Honeytrap logged 214,000 events, and the busiest attacker sent every single one of its 27,140 packets to the same port, which was port 5038.

![Honeytrap Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Honeytrap%20Elastic%20Dashboard.png)

![Honeytrap Top Attacker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Honeytrap%20Top%20Attacker.png)

Port 5038 belongs to the Asterisk Manager Interface. Asterisk is the software that many small and mid sized businesses use to run their phone systems, and port 5038 is its administrative control channel. Anyone with access to it can place calls, read the dial plan, and change the configuration. In practical terms that means full control of the phone system.

### The mystery

The attack pattern was oddly perfect.

![Honeytrap Actor Histogram](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Honeytrap%20Top%20Attacker%20Histogram.png)

It held a flat line at roughly 850 events per interval from July 18 through July 21 with no variation and no bursts, and then it stopped abruptly. Nothing followed.

A line that flat means software rather than a person, specifically software with a fixed speed limit built into it. My first thought was that the machine had been shut down or the hosting provider had cut it off.

That conclusion was wrong, and the way I found out is the useful part.

### Someone else was watching the same attacker

AbuseIPDB showed reports from Enthec Solutions, a German security company that runs its own sensors. They had been reporting this same address for port 5038 activity day by day.

| Date | Attempts they saw in 24 hours |
|---|---|
| July 18 | 504 |
| July 19 | 6,769 |
| July 20 | 6,776 |
| July 21 | 3,274 |
| July 22 onward | none |

That is my graph reproduced by a sensor on another continent. It shows the same start, the same steady middle, and the same ending, with the final day at roughly half volume, which fits a campaign that stopped partway through the day.

Two unrelated sensors agreeing on the beginning, middle, and end of a campaign is about as solid as this kind of confirmation gets.

The same address was then reported again seven hours before I looked, this time by a honeypot in the United States, for VoIP call fraud rather than port 5038 activity. An Australian sensor had also logged roughly 9,900 fraud attempts from it back in early June.

So the machine never went away. The operator finished scanning for phone systems and moved on to actually abusing them. The port 5038 activity stopped because that phase of the work was complete.

I would have concluded the opposite if I had only looked at my own data.

### A rented machine rather than an owned one

The reverse DNS name for this address ends in powered.by.rdp.sh. That belongs to a commercial service that rents anonymous Windows machines by the hour.

Shodan confirms the picture. The machine runs Windows Server 2022 with ports open for web hosting, file sharing, remote desktop, remote management, and message queuing. The company that owns the address block is 1337 Services GmbH in Hamburg, which is the same corporate entity behind Njalla, a well known privacy focused hosting and domain provider.

So this attacker is a customer renting anonymous computing power. That explains something that puzzled me at first. This address has only 47 abuse reports from 15 sources, compared to 350,487 reports from 547 sources for the Cowrie attacker. One rented machine working a selected target list makes far less noise than a dedicated attack fleet.

The two operators run completely different kinds of businesses.

| Comparison | Investigation 1 | Investigation 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Operating system | Ubuntu Linux, SSH only | Windows Server 2022, six services open |
| Abuse reports | 350,487 from 547 sources | 47 from 15 sources |
| GreyNoise | Malicious, verified | Never observed scanning |
| Cisco Talos blocklist | Listed since September 2025 | Not listed |
| Model | Owns disposable machines | Rents anonymous ones |

### An attacker with worse security than its targets

This is the part I find genuinely funny. This machine spends its days scanning the internet for exposed administrative interfaces, and yet it has file sharing, remote desktop, remote management, and message queuing all exposed to the internet itself. Message queuing in particular is the service behind a known family of remote code execution vulnerabilities.

### One note on geography

Cisco Talos places this machine in Conroe, Texas, which is about 40 miles from my sensor, and names a Dutch company as the operator. AbuseIPDB and Shodan both say New York City. The company is German. The registration says United States.

Talos is reading outdated information. Nobody should interpret this as a local attacker. It is a good example of why location data alone never supports a conclusion about who is behind an attack.

## Investigation 3: The Regular

**IP address:** 108.181.56.189
**Honeypot:** Sentrypeer, the fake phone system
**Events:** 87,701, which is 46 percent of everything Sentrypeer saw

> **Assessment, Confidence High.** This is phone bill fraud targeting a single UK number, and it is the same operation I documented in WTIR-05.
> **Evidence.** Captured call data including the dialed number, source port and timing analysis across 87,701 events, 519 abuse reports from 139 sources, and direct comparison with two earlier reports.

This is the third time this address has appeared in the series. It showed up in WTIR-03, again in WTIR-05, and now again in WTIR-06.

![Sentrypeer Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Sentrypeer%20Elastic%20Dashboard.png)

![Sentrypeer Top Attacker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Sentrypeer%20Top%20Attacker.png)

The campaign was already running when my reporting window opened. It held steady through midday on July 19, then faded out, with one lone event on July 22.

![Sentrypeer Histogram](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Sentrypeer%20Histogram.png)

### What it was actually trying to do

Sentrypeer captures the contents of VoIP call attempts, and this is where the evidence becomes unusually clear.

Every single request was an INVITE, which is the message that starts a phone call. There was not one OPTIONS or REGISTER message, and those are what a scanner sends to find out whether a phone system exists and what it supports. Skipping them means the attacker already had a target list. They were past the looking stage and straight into trying.

Every call was placed to the same phone number in six different formats, split almost exactly evenly between them.

| Format tried | Style |
|---|---|
| +441292379005 | International standard format |
| 00441292379005 | European international prefix |
| 000441292379005 | A variation on the above |
| 011441292379005 | North American international prefix |
| 441292379005 | Plain national format |
| 900441292379005 | Premium rate style prefix |

The prefix +44 1292 is the area code for Ayr, Scotland.

The six formats matter for a specific reason. Every phone system has rules about how numbers must be dialed, and those rules differ from system to system. By trying all six variations, the attacker guarantees that if a system will place the call at all, one of the six will work.

This is International Revenue Share Fraud. The person behind it earns money whenever calls to that number connect. They break into a company's phone system, make it dial the number repeatedly, and the company receives the bill. It is one of the most common and most profitable forms of telecom crime.

It is also the exact same number I documented in WTIR-05 six weeks ago. Same address, same target, same method. The operation paused and restarted, but nothing about it changed.

The tool also disguises itself on every request. Each call attempt claimed to come from a different phone model, including the Akuvox X915, the Mitel 6940, the Poly SoundPoint, the Avaya IP Office, Asterisk PBX, Kamailio, and dozens more. All of them are real and ordinary products. The tool rotates through 50 or more of these names specifically so that anyone filtering on suspicious equipment sees nothing unusual.

### The thing it forgot to hide

The disguise is only skin deep. Underneath it, this tool leaves a fingerprint you cannot miss.

Every time a machine sends traffic it picks a numbered door on its own end to send from, which is called a source port. Normal software picks these more or less at random out of about 28,000 possibilities, so traffic from a legitimate system spreads across thousands of different numbers.

This tool used 48 source ports in total across 87,701 events, and 91 percent of the traffic came from just eight consecutive ones.

| Source port | Share of traffic |
|---|---|
| 49183 | 11.6 percent |
| 49179 | 11.5 percent |
| 49180 | 11.4 percent |
| 49176 | 11.4 percent |
| 49178 | 11.4 percent |
| 49181 | 11.4 percent |
| 49177 | 11.4 percent |
| 49182 | 11.3 percent |
| Everything else | 7.2 percent |

Eight ports in a row, each carrying almost exactly one eighth of the traffic. That is not random. The tool cycles through a fixed set of eight in order, over and over again.

The timing is just as rigid. Consecutive events from the same port landed 25 seconds apart every time, at 11:48:56, 11:49:21, 11:49:46, 11:50:12, and 11:50:37. That is a hard coded delay rather than network variation.

For comparison, this attacker averaged 1,827 events per source port. The attacker in Investigation 2 averaged 5.6, and the attacker in Investigation 1 averaged 12.6.

So the operator put real effort into disguising what the traffic looked like and none at all into disguising how it behaved. That tells you what they expect defenders to be watching. It also means a detection rule built on the port pattern and the timing would still catch them after they change addresses, which a simple block list would not.

### A machine that was built to attack

Shodan shows Windows Server 2022 with only remote desktop exposed, and a computer name of WIN-49EHHOVCLON. That is the random name Windows generates during installation, and it was never changed.

The machine's security certificate was created on June 21, 2026. The first abuse report against it is dated June 22, 2026.

That is a gap of one day. This machine was set up and put to work immediately. In roughly five weeks it has collected 519 abuse reports from 139 organizations at 100 percent confidence, with the most recent report filed 37 minutes before I looked.

That also explains why it appeared as a new attacker in WTIR-03 back in late June. It genuinely was new.

It is worth noting that this machine and the one in Investigation 2 are both Windows Server 2022 systems with remote desktop exposed, and both are running phone fraud. Two of this week's three attackers rent Windows machines rather than running their own Linux infrastructure.

### What changed since last time

In WTIR-05 I caught this machine with its VoIP scanning software visible on Shodan, routing calls through Korean and Chinese carriers. Now Shodan shows only remote desktop. Either the software no longer accepts incoming connections, or the operator tightened things up after five weeks of complaints. The attacks continued regardless.

### Two vendors, opposite conclusions

Cisco Talos rates this address as Neutral and has not blocklisted it. Talos also names the wrong owner, reporting a Canadian telecom that transferred this block to Psychz Networks back in 2023.

AbuseIPDB meanwhile has it at 100 percent confidence with 139 organizations reporting VoIP fraud, and sensors in Norway, Germany, France, the Netherlands, Austria, the UK, and the United States were still logging it through July 24.

Compare that to Investigation 1, where Talos blocklisted the address ten months ago. The evidence is similar in both cases but the responses are opposite. It is worth remembering that no single reputation source is complete.

There is one unconfirmed item. OTX AlienVault lists this address as exploiting Shellshock, which is a 2014 web server vulnerability. That has nothing to do with phone fraud. Either this machine runs a second unrelated operation, or somebody tagged it incorrectly. I am noting it rather than claiming it.

### Credit where it is due

Psychz Networks, which operates this address, has genuinely good paperwork. Their registration record includes a documented abuse process, specific instructions on what evidence to include, separate contacts for abuse, network operations, legal requests, and peering, and a published geographic feed.

That is the opposite of the Gmail address in Investigation 1. Even so, a five week old customer machine on their network is running global phone fraud with 519 complaints against it.

The honest conclusion is that having a working abuse desk is not the same thing as acting on it.

Three attackers this week sat on three providers with three different kinds of accountability.

Techoff shows no real accountability at all. It uses a Gmail contact, scattered registration details, and has spent ten months on a blocklist with no effect.

1337 Services sells anonymity as the product. It maintains real contacts, but the business model is untraceable computing.

Psychz has a professional process with mixed results. Everything is documented properly and the abuse continues anyway.

## Investigation 4: The Attacker Who Was Not There

**IP address:** 209.126.127.69
**Honeypot:** ConPot, the fake industrial control system
**Events shown:** 42,833
**Events actual:** 1

This is the investigation that matters most, and it is not really about an attacker.

ConPot showed 192,000 events for the week, which ranked it third. Its top attacker showed 42,833 events, making it the second biggest single attacker number on the entire sensor.

![ConPot Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Conpot%20Elastic%20Dashboard.png)

![ConPot Top Attacker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Conpot%20Top%20Attacker.png)

Those are real log lines. They are not attacks. Working out the difference took most of a day.

### The thing that looked wrong

Every other attacker this week produced a shape of some kind, whether a sawtooth, a flat line, or a fade out. This one produced a single vertical spike with nothing on either side of it.

![ConPot Actor Histogram](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-06/WTIR-06%20Conpot%20Top%20Attacker%20Histogram.png)

So I opened the underlying records. Every single one carried the identical timestamp down to the microsecond, reading 2026-07-23T12:40:56.887372. They also shared the same session ID and the same source port.

That works out to 42,833 events in one microsecond, which is about 42 billion packets per second. My home internet connection cannot receive that. Something was wrong with the number rather than with the attacker.

### My first theory, and why it was wrong

I assumed the log shipping software had accidentally read the same log file twice and indexed everything a second time. That is a well known failure mode and it would produce exactly this pattern.

To test the theory I logged into the sensor and checked the honeypot's own raw log file, meaning the one written before any of the processing pipeline touches it.

```bash
zcat conpot_IEC104.json.3.gz conpot_IEC104.json.2.gz | grep -c '209.126.127.69'
42833
```

The 42,833 lines were already there in the honeypot's own log. The processing pipeline had nothing to do with it. My theory was wrong.

I spent several hours on that theory before the evidence killed it. Checking the raw files earlier would have saved most of that time.

### What was really happening

The answer was in the attack itself.

The incoming request was an SNMP GetBulk. SNMP is the protocol used to monitor network equipment, and routers, switches, and industrial controllers all report their status through it. A GetBulk request asks a device to return an entire branch of its information tree in a single response.

This particular request asked for the branch at 1.3.6.1.4.1, which is the root of all vendor specific equipment data. In plain terms, the request said tell me everything you know.

ConPot writes one log line for every single item it returns. Every one of those lines gets stamped with the time the original request arrived.

One request came in. ConPot walked the tree and wrote 42,833 log lines going out. Every line is identical because they all came from the same request at the same instant.

```
{"timestamp": "2026-07-23T12:40:56.887372", "src_ip": "209.126.127.69",
 "src_port": 54527, "dst_port": 161, "data_type": "snmp",
 "request": {"oid": "(1, 3, 6, 1, 4, 1)"}, "event_type": "SNMPv2 Bulk"}
```

That line repeats 42,832 more times, byte for byte.

So 209.126.127.69 sent my sensor exactly one packet, and my dashboard showed it 42,833 times.

Here is the part worth being precise about. ConPot logged correctly, because it really did return 42,833 values and it wrote a line for each one. The processing pipeline indexed those lines correctly. Nothing in the software failed.

The problem is in the presentation. The dashboard panel is labeled **Honeypot Attacks**, and what it actually counts is log records. For most honeypots those two things are the same, so the label holds. For ConPot answering a GetBulk request they are not the same at all, and the panel reports 192,000 attacks when 3,231 requests arrived. The Attacker Source IP table has the same flaw, which is how a machine that sent one packet came to outrank machines that sent 227.

A panel labeled Attacks that displays something other than attacks is misleading regardless of who is reading it. That said, the responsibility for catching it was mine. I trusted a label without confirming that it held for that particular data source, and confirming it would have taken about ten seconds.

Both halves of that are worth stating, because both halves needed fixing.

### Checking the other three

If I had misread ConPot's numbers, I had to assume I might be misreading the others as well. Cowrie, Honeytrap, and Sentrypeer do not record session IDs, so I checked source ports instead. Real connections come from many different ports, while records generated by a single interaction all share one port.

| Honeypot | Records | Unique source ports | Records per port | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cowrie | 219,000 | 17,385 | 12.6 | Real |
| Honeytrap | 214,000 | 38,069 | 5.6 | Real |
| Sentrypeer | 190,000 | Wide spread | About 13 | Real |
| ConPot top entry | 42,833 | 1 | 42,833 | Distorted |

Cowrie's ratio of 12.6 is expected because it logs several events per SSH session. Honeytrap's 5.6 is closer to one record per connection. Neither resembles ConPot's single port and single timestamp pattern.

Their attack graphs support the same conclusion. Real traffic has a shape, and a logging artifact does not.

### What I changed

The fix has two halves, because the problem had two halves. One is a dashboard that reports the wrong thing. The other is a process that did not check.

**I built a corrected dashboard.** The default T-Pot dashboard remains untouched so that platform updates do not overwrite my work. Alongside it I now run a second dashboard that counts what the labels claim to count.

1. ConPot panels count unique sessions rather than log records. Had this been in place first, 209.126.127.69 would have appeared as a single event and never entered the report at all.
2. Panels that count records are labeled as record counts. Panels that count attacks count attacks. Where a honeypot logs one interaction as many records, the panel says so.
3. A telemetry health panel sits at the top, showing record count against session count for every honeypot side by side. Each one now has a documented baseline, with ConPot at roughly 55 records per request, Cowrie at 12.6, Sentrypeer at 13, and Honeytrap at 5.6. A number that drifts from its baseline is now visible immediately rather than discovered a day into an investigation.
4. An alert fires when any of those ratios moves away from its baseline, so I find out without having to look.

**I added data verification to the weekly process.** Starting with this report, three checks run before any analysis begins, and they take a few minutes in total.

1. I check the physics. Dividing events by elapsed time gives a packet rate, and if that rate exceeds what my connection can physically receive, I stop and investigate. That single check is what caught this problem.
2. I check the shape. Real traffic ramps up, holds, and fades. A lone spike with nothing on either side of it usually points to a logging behavior rather than an attack.
3. I verify against the raw log. For any number that carries weight in a report, I confirm it against the honeypot's own file on disk before it gets published. Elasticsearch sits downstream of the honeypot, and the file on disk is the closest thing I have to ground truth.

That verification step is now written into the methodology at the front of this report, and it will run every week from here forward.

**One note for other T-Pot operators.** This is not a fault unique to my sensor. Anyone running ConPot alongside the stock dashboard is very likely reporting inflated numbers, because the behavior is built into how ConPot logs and how the panels count. It is worth checking.

### The attacker who was not there

There is one more turn to this story. Having established that 209.126.127.69 sent a single packet, I looked into who it actually was.

| Source | Result |
|---|---|
| AbuseIPDB | Zero reports, and not in the database at all |
| VirusTotal | Zero out of 91 vendors flag it |
| Cisco Talos | Not blocklisted, neutral rating, DNS records match properly |
| GreyNoise | Never observed scanning |
| Shodan | A live server with real services running |
| Hurricane Electric cert search | Holds a valid security certificate for a real domain |

Every source came back clean.

The machine is condor2387.startdedicated.com, an ordinary rented server from a German hosting company. Its neighbors in the same address range are normal customer servers with decent reputations. On port 8080 it runs miniSIPServer with a login page, which means it is a small business phone system.

> **Assessment, Confidence Medium.** The evidence fits an SNMP reflection attack. In this kind of attack someone sends requests to thousands of internet devices while forging the return address so that it points at their real victim. Every device that answers sends its reply to the victim instead of the sender. The attacker sends one small packet and the victim gets buried in responses.

My ConPot was one of the devices that answered. That single GetBulk request was almost certainly not sent by 209.126.127.69 at all. Someone else sent it with that address forged onto it, so that my honeypot would fire its response at a small phone system in Missouri that had nothing to do with me.

Confidence is Medium rather than High because forged traffic cannot be proven from outside. The assessment rests on several pieces of evidence pointing the same direction, including a single packet, a request designed to generate the largest possible reply, a supposed attacker with a perfectly clean record everywhere, and a victim profile that fits, since VoIP systems are frequent targets of this kind of attack.

One telling detail supports it. GreyNoise marked the Investigation 1 attacker as not spoofable because it watched that machine complete real handshakes. It has never observed 209.126.127.69 doing anything at all, which is exactly what you would expect from an address that is not really sending anything.

There is a part of this that redeems the whole detour. Those 42,833 log lines from one request are a directly measured amplification factor. One small request produced a response tens of thousands of items long. That is normally a statistic you cite from a paper, and I measured it on my own equipment by accident.

One last note is worth adding. ConPot was running its power grid controller profile at the time, and the SNMP service attached to that fake device is what answered. Someone hunting for any device that would amplify their attack happened to hit a machine pretending to be industrial control equipment. Real substation and factory floor gear commonly runs SNMP for monitoring and is rarely firewalled, which makes industrial networks an attractive pool of amplifiers.

## Detection Opportunities

Addresses change but behavior does not. An attacker can rent a new machine in ten minutes, while rewriting their tool to behave differently takes real work, so patterns are more durable than addresses. These are the patterns from this week that would still catch these operators after they move.

- **Watch for SSH usernames that give away the target.** Alert on login attempts using solana, sol, or solv. Generic attack tools do not contain these names, so their presence means someone is specifically hunting cryptocurrency infrastructure.

- **Watch for VoIP calls with no introduction.** Alert when a single source sends INVITE messages with no OPTIONS or REGISTER beforehand within a five minute window. Legitimate phones introduce themselves first. An attacker going straight to call attempts already has your system on a list.

- **Watch for the same number in multiple formats.** Alert when one source tries the same destination in three or more dialing formats. There is no legitimate reason to do this. It is fraud testing your dial plan.

- **Watch for a small block of repeating source ports.** Flag sources that spread traffic evenly across eight or fewer consecutive ports, especially when the timing between packets is consistent. This detection survives an address change and defeats the disguise entirely.

- **Watch for your own equipment acting as an amplifier.** Alert on incoming GetBulk requests for 1.3.6.1.4.1 or other large branches. More importantly, watch your outbound SNMP volume to any single destination, because that is the signal that your equipment is being used to attack someone else. Monitoring that only looks at inbound traffic will never see it.

- **Watch your own data for ratio drift.** Alert when the ratio of log records to sessions moves away from the baseline for a given source. The baselines are roughly 55 to 1 for ConPot, 13 to 1 for Sentrypeer, 12.6 to 1 for Cowrie, and 5.6 to 1 for Honeytrap.

## MITRE ATT&CK Mapping

MITRE ATT&CK is a public catalog of the things attackers actually do, organized into stages such as finding a target, getting in, staying in, and causing damage. Each entry has a code like T1110.003. Security teams use it as shared vocabulary so that password spraying means the same thing at every organization. Mapping observed behavior to it is how you compare this week against last week, or your findings against someone else's.

A word on how to read the confidence column. High means I watched the behavior happen and at least one outside source agreed with my reading. Medium means the indirect evidence is strong but one piece cannot be verified. Forged traffic cannot be proven from outside, a failed login does not prove what the attacker planned to do next, and I cannot see a hosting provider's customer records to confirm who rented what.

On that last point, I map the Techoff machine as acquired infrastructure rather than compromised infrastructure. Everything about it fits a machine obtained for the purpose, including one service running, an outdated base image, and a surrounding address block where 77 of 256 hosts carry poor reputations. A hacked legitimate server would normally still be running whatever it was built to do. This one does nothing but attack.

## Defensive Recommendations

- **SNMP should never be reachable from the internet.** Default community strings such as public and private are indefensible. Where SNMP must be exposed, limit how fast it responds and how much data a single request can return. This matters most on industrial networks, where monitoring equipment is common and firewalls are rare.

- **Source address filtering belongs at the network edge.** Reflection attacks only work because forged return addresses are allowed to cross provider boundaries. This is the actual fix, and it belongs to network operators rather than end users. The relevant standard is BCP 38.

- **VoIP systems need layered protection.** Restrict access to known callers where you can. Do not rely on filtering by equipment name, because this week's attacker rotates through 50 legitimate ones. Watch for call attempts that arrive with no registration first. Set international dialing limits and per extension call caps, since this kind of fraud only pays when the calls connect.

- **Port 5038 should never face the internet.** Access to the Asterisk Manager Interface is effectively administrative control of the phone system.

- **SSH should use keys rather than passwords.** Turn off password login entirely. If you run cryptocurrency infrastructure, this week's evidence shows that your service account names are being targeted by name.

- **Consider blocking whole address ranges** for 195.178.110.0/24 and 192.159.99.0/24. In both cases the abuse spans the entire block rather than a single machine.

- **Check your own tools before you trust them.** A record count is not an event count until you have verified that it is.

## Intelligence Gaps

A gaps section is where an analyst writes down what they could not figure out. It matters because the alternative, which is quietly leaving those things out, makes a report look more certain than it is.

Here is what I could not determine this week.

- **Where the Solana username list came from.** Validator operator lists are publicly available, but I cannot confirm that the attacker used one.

- **Who is behind any of this.** I can identify hosting providers and rented machines, but I cannot get past them to a person or a group, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

- **Whether the reflection victim was actually attacked.** I observed one forged request reaching my sensor, and I have no visibility into what the target experienced.

- **Whether the Shellshock report on 108.181.56.189 is real.** It does not fit anything else about that machine and may simply be a mislabeled entry.

- **Whether WTIR-01 through WTIR-05 reported ConPot the same wrong way.** Compressed logs go back 30 days, so a partial check is possible and I intend to run it.

- **What happened after July 22.** All three campaigns went quiet before my window closed, and whether they ended, moved, or paused is unknown.

- **Why no malware was captured.** Every campaign I observed involved scanning, credential guessing, or fraud attempts, and none of them delivered a payload.

## What This Week Taught Me

**Changing how you choose changes what you find.** Picking attackers by raw volume would have given me three similar looking high count addresses. Picking one per honeypot gave me three operators with nothing in common, including a global password sprayer running its own machines, a renter working through a two phase plan, and a fraud tool with a careful disguise and a careless signature. That contrast is the actual intelligence, and volume ranking would have hidden all of it.

**Phone fraud is the most persistent thing my sensor sees.** Two of three attackers were VoIP related, and even the reflection victim was a phone system. That makes three reports in a row featuring phone fraud, with the same attacker on its third appearance targeting the same number. Attacks on web servers and SSH rotate constantly. The phone fraud economy is stable, profitable, and shows up every single week with the same infrastructure. If I were setting priorities based on what I actually observe, VoIP would rank far higher than it usually does.

**Your own tools need checking like any other source.** I spent the week carefully cross referencing seven sources on external attackers, catching a four country disagreement, and separating a victim from an attacker. Then I turned that same attention on my own dashboard and found a panel labeled Attacks that was showing me something other than attacks, with a gap of 42,833 to 1 on the attacker I was about to write up.

Two things were wrong at once. The dashboard was reporting log records under a label that promised attacks, which is misleading to anyone who reads it. And I trusted that label without spending ten seconds confirming it held for that data source. I have fixed both. There is now a corrected dashboard that counts what its labels claim, and there is a verification step in the weekly process that runs before any analysis begins.

The broader lesson is that tool output and ground truth are not the same thing, and the space between them is exactly where analytical errors live. That applies to my sensor, and it applies equally to every external source I cite. I already treat AbuseIPDB and Talos as things to corroborate rather than believe. My own dashboard had somehow escaped that standard, purely because it was mine.

My first explanation was wrong as well. I was convinced for hours that it was a log processing bug, and I only ruled it out by going to the raw files on disk. Chasing a wrong theory until the evidence kills it is not wasted effort, but it does argue for reaching the source of truth earlier than I did.

The honest version of this report is smaller than the one my dashboard offered. It is also correct, which matters more.
