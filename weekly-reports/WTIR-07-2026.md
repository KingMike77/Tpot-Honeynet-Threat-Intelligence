## Executive Summary

> **At a Glance**
> - **Total events:** ~876,000 — down ~3% for the third consecutive week
> - **Top 3 countries:** United States, Romania, Netherlands
> - **Recurring actor #1:** 160.119.76.106 (Alsycon B.V./HostUS) — 3rd confirmed appearance, spanning WTIR-02, 05, and 07
> - **Recurring actor #2:** 108.181.56.189 (Psychz Networks) — 4th confirmed appearance, spanning WTIR-03, 05, 06, and 07
> - **ConPot corrected session count:** 2,933 (raw count was 120,000 — a second, distinct logging bug identified and excluded)

This week marks a shift in how this series looks at the data. Rather than asking only "who were the top attackers," WTIR-07 asks what changed week over week — which services gained or lost share, which countries carried the most traffic, and whether any actors from past reports came back.

The honeynet logged roughly 876,000 attack events, continuing a gradual three-week decline from WTIR-05's 933,000 through WTIR-06's 901,000. This report profiles the top attacker in each of the week's three leading countries — the United States, Romania, and the Netherlands — instead of by honeypot as in WTIR-06.

The most significant finding this week is a confirmed, multi-month recurring campaign. **160.119.76.106**, operating under Alsycon B.V./HostUS Solutions (AS49870) in the Netherlands, is the same actor flagged in WTIR-05 as that week's most concerning host — and the same ASN traces back further to WTIR-02's Actor 3 as well. Across all three appearances, the actor has run the same enterprise VPN and firewall exploitation toolkit and carried the same multi-country attribution mismatch.

A second recurring actor, **108.181.56.189** (Psychz Networks), returned again this week running its now-familiar toll-fraud campaign against the same UK phone number first seen in WTIR-05 — its fourth documented appearance in this series.

ConPot's logging duplication bug identified in WTIR-06 recurred in a different form this week, inflating one source's record count roughly 810x. It was identified, verified, and excluded from attacker rankings using the same validation method established last week.

## What I Changed This Week

Every report through WTIR-06 profiled attackers either by overall volume or by top honeypot. This week I changed the lens twice:

- **Trend framing over snapshot framing.** Instead of just reporting the current period's numbers, I pulled the last several weeks' totals and service breakdowns to see what actually changed — a gradual volume decline, a shift toward more Honeytrap traffic, and a mostly-flat ConPot once the logging bug is corrected out.
- **Top attacker by country, not by honeypot.** I identified the top 3 countries by traffic this week — United States, Romania, and the Netherlands — and profiled the leading attacker within each, rather than picking one actor per honeypot service.
- **Standing validation rule for ConPot.** After finding a second, distinct logging-inflation bug this week, I made session-based validation (ranking by unique `id.keyword` rather than raw record count) a permanent step for any ConPot-sourced candidate before it's considered for a top-attacker profile.
- **Cross-report recurrence checks.** Rather than noting a recurring actor only when I happened to recognize an IP, I went back through WTIR-01 through WTIR-06 and checked ASN- and IP-level overlap directly. That's what surfaced the Alsycon/HostUS three-report recurrence — the strongest finding in this report.

## Methodology

This report is based on telemetry collected by a public-facing T-Pot honeynet deployed over a seven-day period (July 25 – August 1, 2026). Data sources include Elastic/Kibana dashboards, Suricata IDS alerts, and T-Pot honeypot logs, cross-referenced against external OSINT: Hurricane Electric's BGP Toolkit (including ARIN, RIPE, and AFRINIC RDAP/WHOIS lookups), Shodan, VirusTotal, AbuseIPDB, GreyNoise, Cisco Talos, and OTX AlienVault.

**Session validation.** ConPot has now shown two independent logging-inflation defects across the last two reporting periods — a per-varbind fan-out bug in WTIR-06 and a duplicate log-write bug this week. Any ConPot-sourced candidate for a top-attacker profile is cross-checked against a unique-session count (`unique_count(id.keyword)`) rather than raw record count before inclusion. Honeypots that don't populate a session ID field are validated instead against a records-per-source-port ratio, benchmarked against clean baselines established in WTIR-06 (Cowrie ~12.6, Honeytrap ~5.6, Sentrypeer ~13).

**Recurrence checks.** Before finalizing the profiles below, each candidate IP and its ASN were checked against every prior WTIR report for overlap, rather than relying on recognizing a familiar IP by memory.

**Country selection.** The top 3 countries were identified by total logged event volume for the reporting week, then the leading source IP within each country was selected for profiling.

**Confidence ratings.** Each profiled actor in this report receives a confidence tier based on how many independent sources corroborate the finding and whether the observation comes from direct honeypot telemetry or an inference drawn from it.

| Tier | Criteria | Example basis |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Directly observed in this honeynet's own telemetry, and corroborated by 2 or more independent external sources | GreyNoise classification + AbuseIPDB report history + WHOIS/RDAP registration data all agreeing |
| **Medium** | Directly observed in this honeynet's telemetry, but corroborated by only one external source, or the external sources disagree on a minor detail | A GreyNoise tag confirms behavior, but AbuseIPDB has too few reports to independently verify scale |
| **Low** | A plausible explanation consistent with the available data, but resting on inference rather than direct confirmation, with a real possibility of an alternate explanation | A hostname or ASN pattern suggests a link between two actors that hasn't been independently verified |

A rating is assigned per finding, not per IP — the same actor can carry a High-confidence rating for "this is a brute-force scanner" while a related but separate claim about that actor (for example, attribution to a specific operator) might only warrant Medium or Low.

### Investigation 3: The Toolkit for Hire — Romania

**193.46.255.112 (UNMANAGED LTD)**

![WTIR-07 Romania Dashboard](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KingMike77/Tpot-Honeynet-Threat-Intelligence/main/Screenshots/week-07/WTIR-07%20Romania%20Dashboard.png)

Hosted by UNMANAGED LTD (AS47890), a UK-registered reseller operating Romanian infrastructure out of Timișoara. GreyNoise identified a broad enterprise edge-device scanning toolkit targeting Fortinet, Palo Alto, Ivanti, F5, Sophos, ConnectWise, CrushFTP, FreePBX, and WordPress — most notably FortiOS Info Disclosure (CVE-2018-13379), Palo Alto PAN-OS Crawler (CVE-2020-2034), and ThinkPHP LFI (CVE-2022-47945).

The Romania-tagged dashboard shows the port spread behind this reach: 22 (SSH), 443 (HTTPS), 6379 (Redis), 9100 (network printer JetDirect), and 25 (SMTP) all appear. The Attacks by Honeypot breakdown for this country shows meaningful traffic against Honeytrap, Cowrie, Ciscoasa, H0neytr4p, Miniprint, Redishoneypot, Dionaea, ConPot, Mailoney, and Dicompot, rather than concentrating on one service.

193.46.255.112's own AbuseIPDB report history confirms the same actor is behind hits across multiple honeypot types specifically, not just the wider country-level traffic. Reporters have independently logged this IP against **ConPot** (FTP Brute-Force category), **Cowrie/SSH** (SSH Port Scan, Brute-Force SSH), a **MySQL port scan** (3306/tcp), **Telnet brute-force**, a **web app attack**, and a **Mailoney honeypot hit**.

That spread — one IP, caught by name across at least five distinct honeypot services in independent reports — is consistent with the broad, multi-protocol scanning behavior GreyNoise's tag list already suggested, rather than a narrow single-service tool.

This CVE/tag combination closely mirrors the toolkit run by the Alsycon/HostUS actor above. WHOIS verification confirms these are unrelated operators — different ASNs, different abuse contacts, different registered addresses. The overlap is best read as evidence of a shared, widely-circulated commodity exploitation-scanning toolkit rather than a connected campaign.

AbuseIPDB shows 156 reports from 87 sources, first reported April 2021 — over four years of documented activity. Shodan shows OpenSSH 7.4 on port 22, a version from 2016 carrying a long list of unpatched CVEs.

**Assessment — Confidence: High.** Long-running (4+ year), broad-spectrum opportunistic scanner running a commodity enterprise-exploit toolset, infrastructurally unrelated to the Alsycon/HostUS actor despite near-identical tooling.

## What This Week Taught Me

The pivot toward trend analysis paid off immediately. Tracking the same ASN across three months surfaced a sustained enterprise VPN/firewall exploitation campaign that no single week's report would have revealed on its own, and the Psychz/IRSF actor's fourth appearance confirms that campaign has now outlasted the entire back half of this series.

I also learned to be more careful about verifying data before treating it as new. During the reporting window's research, I nearly reported a two-month-old screenshot as a live finding, and a dashboard panel silently returned stale results after a filter change. Both were caught, but only because I double-checked rather than assumed. Going forward, I'm treating "confirm the date range is actually current" as a standing step, the same way session-based validation became standing practice after WTIR-06.

The biggest lesson is that recurring infrastructure is worth tracking explicitly, not just noting in passing when it happens to resurface. A one-week snapshot would have shown three unconnected countries and three unconnected actors. Looking back across the full report history showed two of them are chapters in ongoing campaigns.

Worth checking next week: whether 160.119.76.106 is still active, whether the port 5901/VNC scanning trend flagged back in WTIR-04 ever resurfaced, and whether Romania's shared commodity toolkit shows up under a different ASN elsewhere in the data.
